   110→\bfseries
   111→The Rate--Distortion Theory of Bounded Sequential Transduction:\\
   112→A Comparative Syntax for Finite-State Approximation
   113→}
   114→\author{}
   115→\date{}
   116→

   118→\maketitle

   120→%======================================================================
   121→\begin{abstract}
   122→We develop a comparative framework for finite-state approximation of
   123→sequential objects: a rate--distortion theory of bounded sequential
   124→transduction in which the rate is a state budget $M$ and the distortion is a
   125→regime-specific task loss.  Three regimes share one syntax --- histories,
   126→finite-index right congruences, quotient machines, stationary aggregation, and
   127→variational gaps --- and differ in their semantic objects.  Commitment targets
   128→deterministic specifications through deterministic Mealy machines and
   129→Myhill--Nerode residuals.  Retention targets stochastic controlled processes
   130→through stationary controlled unifilar causal machines (controlled
   131→$\epsilon$-machines, whose state update may read the realized output) and
   132→their lumpable quotients.  Grounding targets stochastic channels through
   133→linear finite-rank Hankel realizations, whose resource is operator rank rather
   134→than quotient index; a closed-form finite-budget characterization of exact
   135→symbolic deterministic grounding remains open, but an observable deterministic
   136→floor, its finite-state limit, and certified finite-horizon and finite-section
   137→intervals are established.  Every approximation claim carries an explicit type
   138→signature fixing regime, feasible set, aggregation, support, protocol, and
   139→analytical bridge, so that statements from different regimes are not
   140→conflated.
   141→
   142→The principal exact results are as follows.  The retention full-KL gap admits
   143→an exact finite-state information-bottleneck decomposition over stationary
   144→lumpable quotients, with a controlled analogue, conditional on the input, for
   145→general unifilar machines, and a zero-retention threshold at the stationary
   146→support size of distinct predictive states in the input-driven theory and at
   147→the coarsest unifilar-lumpable refinement of the predictive-kernel partition
   148→in general.  For finite output alphabets a global spectral converse holds in
   149→probability coordinates with sharp constant $1$; under uniform interiority a
   150→corresponding bound holds in a fixed minimal natural-parameter chart, and the
   151→interiority hypothesis cannot be removed.  The unrestricted linear finite-rank
   152→Hankel relaxation gap is $\Dunres(M)=\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu)$ by
   153→Eckart--Young--Mirsky, while the Hankel-structured gap is bounded below by
   154→$\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu)$ and equals it under an Adamjan--Arov--Krein
   155→Hardy-space embedding --- unconditional for one-letter alphabets, conditional
   156→on a multiletter Nehari/AAK-type theorem otherwise, which is not established
   157→here.  A superpolynomial, sub-exponential determinism gap separates formal
   158→Hankel rank from deterministic state complexity.  Retention is NP-complete in
   159→the Gaussian quadratic restricted regime and NP-hard under a promise with
   160→APX-hardness for unrestricted full KL.  Mistake complexity is $\Theta(M\log
   161→M)$ in the reset-word, persistent-stream, and active residual-identification
   162→protocols, with agnostic regret $\Theta(\sqrt{TM\log M})$.
   163→
   164→The framework separates shared syntax from regime-specific analysis: a typed
   165→variational schema; structural monotonicity and zero thresholds; a conditional
   166→response-operator converse and Schatten template; adaptive oracle
   167→inequalities with a matching minimax lower bound under explicit floors; and a
   168→linear second-order Price-of-Safety surrogate with a Ky Fan majorization law.
   169→A vertex correspondence organizes retention, commitment, and grounding by
   170→R\'enyi/Schatten labels $\{0,1,\infty\}$ as a formal consistency rather
   171→than a derivation, and no analytic theorem depends on it.  An independence
   172→theorem shows that the three regimes' obstructions vary independently, so no
   173→cross-regime ordering holds.  The average-case theory uses stationary
   174→Ces\`aro or discounted prefix aggregation throughout, finite prefixes not
   175→defining a probability measure over all lengths.  The manuscript identifies a
   176→small set of sharply posed open research programs in place of a single
   177→undifferentiated open problem of exact symbolic deterministic grounding.
   178→\end{abstract}

   181→%======================================================================
   182→\section{Introduction: The Bounded Sequential Generator and Its Regimes}
   183→\label{sec:intro}
   184→%======================================================================
   185→
   186→This manuscript develops a comparative theory of finite-state approximation
   187→for sequential objects.  The resource is memory, measured by the number of
   188→internal states or, equivalently, by the index of a finite right congruence on
   189→histories.  The cost is a regime-specific distortion functional: Boolean
   190→mismatch for commitment, KL divergence for retention, operator-norm distance
   191→for linear grounding relaxations, and related quantitative costs.
   192→
   193→The framework may be viewed as a rate--distortion theory of bounded sequential
   194→transduction.  The rate is the finite-state budget $M$.  The distortion is the
   195→task loss induced by the regime.
   196→
   197→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   198→\subsection{Bounded sequential generators}
   199→\label{subsec:bsg-intro}
   200→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   201→
   202→A \textbf{Bounded Sequential Generator} (BSG) is a deterministic finite-state
   203→transducer, i.e.\ a Mealy machine,
   204→\[
   205→\mathcal A=(\mathcal S,s_0,\delta,\lambda),
   206→\qquad
   207→|\mathcal S|\le M,
   208→\]
   209→with finite input alphabet $\mathcal I$, finite output alphabet $\mathcal O$,
   210→transition map
   211→\[
   212→\delta:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal S,
   213→\]
   214→and output map
   215→\[
   216→\lambda:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal O.
   217→\]
   218→It defines a synchronous causal function
   219→\[
   220→F_{\mathcal A}:\mathcal I^{\mathbb N}\to\mathcal O^{\mathbb N}.
   221→\]
   222→
   223→The deterministic Mealy BSG is the machine model for the commitment regime.
   224→In that regime, the target is a deterministic specification, a safety
   225→property, or a deterministic strategic rule, and the question is whether a
   226→finite-state machine implements or enforces it exactly or approximately.  The
   227→exact zero-error theory is governed by Myhill--Nerode residual equivalence.
   228→
   229→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   230→\subsection{Three approximation regimes}
   231→\label{subsec:three-regimes}
   232→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   233→
   234→The manuscript studies three regimes.  They share a finite-state syntax but
   235→differ in their semantic objects.  The framework does not reduce the three
   236→regimes to a single machine category.
   237→
   238→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2.2em]
   239→\item \textbf{Commitment.}
   240→The target is a deterministic residual function.  A history $u$ determines a
   241→residual continuation map
   242→\[
   243→F_u(w)=F(uw)_{|u|+1:|u|+|w|}.
   244→\]
   245→Two histories are equivalent if they induce the same residual function.  The
   246→minimal exact state complexity is the Myhill--Nerode index
   247→\[
   248→\kappa_{\det}(F)
   249→=
   250→\operatorname{index}(\sim_F).
   251→\]
   252→The exact commitment gap is a threshold phenomenon:
   253→\[
   254→\Com(M)=0
   255→\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
   256→M\ge\kappa_{\det}(F).
   257→\]
   258→
   259→\item \textbf{Retention.}
   260→The target is a stochastic controlled process whose predictive structure is
   261→encoded by causal states.  Each positive-mass causal state $s\in\Splus$
   262→carries a predictive distribution $P_s$ over future outputs.  An $M$-state
   263→approximation merges causal states into at most $M$ lumped machine states
   264→through a stationary lumpable quotient.
   265→
   266→The full-KL retention gap is
   267→\[
   268→\RetKL(M)
   269→=
   270→\min_{\substack{
   271→\phi:\Splus\to\mathcal K\ \text{lumpable}\\
   272→|\mathcal K|\le M
   273→}}
   274→\sum_{k\in\mathcal K}
   275→\sum_{s:\phi(s)=k}
   276→\pi_s
   277→D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_s\|\bar P_k),
   278→\]
   279→where
   280→\[
   281→\bar P_k
   282→=
   283→\frac{
   284→\sum_{s:\phi(s)=k}\pi_s P_s
   285→}{
   286→\sum_{s:\phi(s)=k}\pi_s
   287→}.
   288→\]
   289→In synchronized machines, lumpable quotients of $\Splus$ correspond to
   290→finite-index right congruences on sufficiently long histories.  In the general
   291→unifilar model the causal-state update reads the realized output, the feasible
   292→quotients are the unifilar-lumpable ones, and the input-driven synchronized
   293→subclass recovers right congruences on input histories.
   294→
   295→When the predictive family is restricted to common-covariance Gaussians, one
   296→obtains a quadratic restricted gap
   297→\[
   298→\RetQuad(M).
   299→\]
   300→The full-KL gap and the quadratic restricted gap are distinct objects.
   301→
   302→\emph{Positioning.}  The retention chapter extends the causal-state program of
   303→computational mechanics \cite{shalizi2001} from output processes to
   304→controlled, input-driven transductions: the objects approximated are lumpable
   305→quotients of a \emph{controlled} unifilar causal machine rather than
   306→partitions of a stationary process, and the feasibility notion of unifilar
   307→lumpability is correspondingly stricter than ordinary lumpability.  Unlike the
   308→information-bottleneck-for-causal-states line
   309→\cite{shalizicrutchfield2002,marzencrutchfield2014}, which characterizes the
   310→optimal causal statistic, and unlike the Kullback--Leibler aggregation line for
   311→regular Markov chains \cite{geiger2015,geiger2026}, which computes the optimal
   312→reduction, the manuscript tracks the full budget-$M$ gap curve: the zero
   313→threshold at the stable kernel refinement, NP-hardness below it, and spectral
   314→lower certificates from the predictive covariance, rather than only the
   315→optimum.
   316→
   317→\item \textbf{Grounding.}
   318→The target is a stochastic channel or residual kernel.  The spectral converse
   319→uses linear finite-rank realizations rather than deterministic Mealy
   320→machines.  The relevant object is a Hankel operator $H_\nu$ built from an
   321→impulse-response function.
   322→
   323→The unrestricted linear finite-rank Hankel relaxation gap is
   324→\[
   325→\Dunres(M)
   326→=
   327→\inf_{\operatorname{rank}B\le M}
   328→\norm{H_\nu-B}_{\mathrm{op}},
   329→\]
   330→and satisfies
   331→\[
   332→\Dunres(M)
   333→=
   334→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu)
   335→\]
   336→by Eckart--Young--Mirsky \cite{eckart1936,mirsky1960}.
   337→
   338→The Hankel-structured finite-rank realization gap is
   339→\[
   340→\DHankstr(M)
   341→=
   342→\inf_{\substack{
   343→\operatorname{rank}B\le M\\
   344→B\ \text{Hankel}
   345→}}
   346→\norm{H_\nu-B}_{\mathrm{op}},
   347→\]
   348→and satisfies
   349→\[
   350→\DHankstr(M)
   351→\ge
   352→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu).
   353→\]
   354→Equality in the Hankel-structured problem requires additional structure
   355→(Theorems~\ref{thm:aak-equality} and~\ref{thm:aak-multiletter}).  The
   356→resource $M$ here is a finite-rank budget on a bounded operator, not the
   357→index of a right congruence; Section~\ref{subsec:grounding-instantiation} and
   358→the master scope of Section~\ref{subsec:meta-scope} record this distinction
   359→explicitly, and Definition~\ref{def:symbolic-grounding-gap} below supplies a
   360→quotient-typed \emph{symbolic} grounding gap, over deterministic Mealy
   361→machines, whose resource is again a right-congruence index and which is
   362→therefore the object genuinely comparable to commitment and retention.  A
   363→closed-form finite-budget value for that symbolic gap remains open; certified
   364→bounds are given in
   365→Propositions~\ref{prop:grounding-finite-section},~\ref{prop:symbolic-finite-horizon}
   366→and Theorem~\ref{thm:observable-floor}.
   367→
   368→\emph{Positioning.}  The grounding chapter transports the
   369→Adamjan--Arov--Krein program for weighted automata
   370→\cite{balle2021,lacroce2024} from weighted languages to transductions.  The
   371→object approximated is the Hankel operator of a channel on the joint alphabet
   372→rather than that of a weighted language; the unrestricted and
   373→Hankel-restricted feasible sets are separated explicitly, the
   374→Eckart--Young--Mirsky value being available for the former and only the
   375→singular-value lower bound for the latter; and the published one-letter
   376→results extend to the multiletter setting only conditionally
   377→(Theorem~\ref{thm:aak-multiletter}, Open Problem~\ref{open:hankel-multiletter}),
   378→with grounded finite-section certificates and the zero-threshold
   379→characterization $\rank(H)\le M$ standing in for the exact minimization values
   380→of the one-letter theory.
   381→\end{enumerate}
   382→
   383→The three regimes share a \textbf{typed finite-memory budget} rather than a
   384→single literal resource.  For commitment and retention (and for the symbolic
   385→grounding gap of Definition~\ref{def:symbolic-grounding-gap}), the budget $M$
   386→is the index of a finite right congruence on histories.  For the
   387→\emph{unrestricted linear} grounding relaxation, the budget $M$ is instead the
   388→rank of a finite-rank operator approximating a Hankel operator.  These two
   389→notions of budget are related --- both cap the ``resolution'' of the
   390→approximant --- but they are not asserted equivalent: a rank-$M$ operator is
   391→not in general reachable through an $M$-state quotient, and an $M$-state
   392→quotient does not in general act on the joint alphabet the way a rank-$M$
   393→Hankel truncation does.  Section~\ref{subsec:meta-scope} makes this typed
   394→picture precise as four pillars common to all three regimes.
   395→
   396→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   397→\subsection{The shared finite-state schema}
   398→\label{subsec:shared-schema-intro}
   399→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   400→
   401→The shared combinatorial resource is the index of a finite quotient of a
   402→regime-typed history system (Definition~\ref{def:history-system}, developed in
   403→Section~\ref{subsec:history-actions}).
   404→Commitment quotients live on input histories $\mathcal I^*$ with a total right
   405→action; unifilar retention quotients live on feasible joint input--output
   406→histories with a pruned action, in the support-relative sense of
   407→Definition~\ref{def:support-right-cong}; grounding quotients live on the joint
   408→alphabet $\Sigma=\mathcal I\times\mathcal O$.  For the commitment regime, and
   409→for the input-driven retention subclass, the classical form is the following;
   410→Remark~\ref{rem:regime-history-instances} tabulates the three instantiations.
   411→
   412→Let $\mathcal I^*$ denote the set of finite input histories.  A right
   413→congruence $\sim$ on $\mathcal I^*$, in the standard sense of automata theory
   414→\cite{sakarovitch2009}, is an equivalence relation satisfying
   415→\[
   416→u\sim v
   417→\implies
   418→ux\sim vx
   419→\qquad
   420→\forall x\in\mathcal I.
   421→\]
   422→Equivalently, the quotient $\mathcal I^*/\!\sim$ carries a well-defined
   423→deterministic transition
   424→\[
   425→[u]\cdot x=[ux].
   426→\]
   427→The index of $\sim$ is the number of equivalence classes.  This index is the
   428→finite-state budget.
   429→
   430→A target sequential object may be represented as a trajectory
   431→\[
   432→\delta:\mathcal I^*\to\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R),
   433→\]
   434→where $\mathbf R$ is a regime-specific residual category.  An $M$-state
   435→approximation factors $\delta$ through a finite quotient:
   436→\[
   437→\mathcal I^*
   438→\longrightarrow
   439→\mathcal I^*/\!\sim
   440→\longrightarrow
   441→\mathbf R,
   442→\qquad
   443→\operatorname{index}(\sim)\le M.
   444→\]
   445→The associated variational gap is
   446→\[
   447→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
   448→=
   449→\inf_{\substack{\sim,\Psi\\ \operatorname{index}(\sim)\le M}}
   450→\mathcal L(\delta,\Psi\circ Q).
   451→\]
   452→
   453→\begin{remark}[Scope of the Quotient Display]
   454→\label{rem:quotient-display-scope}
   455→The display above is the quotient-typed instance of the schema, and it is the
   456→literal resource for commitment, for retention, and for the symbolic
   457→grounding gap of Definition~\ref{def:symbolic-grounding-gap}.  The
   458→\emph{unrestricted linear} grounding relaxation of
   459→Theorem~\ref{thm:spectral-grounding} instead instantiates the schema with a
   460→finite-rank feasible set,
   461→\[
   462→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
   463→=
   464→\inf_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\le M}
   465→\mathcal L(\delta,B),
   466→\]
   467→a genuinely different budget type.  Section~\ref{subsec:meta-scope} records
   468→both instances as coordinates of one typed variational schema without
   469→asserting that the two budgets coincide.
   470→\end{remark}
   471→
   472→Each regime attaches its own per-state semantic object:
   473→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
   474→\item \textbf{commitment:} a residual function;
   475→\item \textbf{retention:} a predictive distribution or exponential-family
   476→parameter;
   477→\item \textbf{grounding:} an operator, kernel, or linear realization
   478→coordinate.
   479→\end{itemize}
   480→
   481→The shared theorems concern the quotient structure and the index of right
   482→congruences.  The analytical engines---Myhill--Nerode theory, information
   483→bottleneck, Hankel approximation, game-theoretic commitment, online learning,
   484→and operator algebra---are regime-specific.
   485→
   486→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   487→\subsection{Stationary aggregation and type-correct costs}
   488→\label{subsec:stationary-intro}
   489→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   490→
   491→The average-case theory uses stationary aggregation.  For each fixed length
   492→$n$, a stationary process assigns total mass one to the prefixes of length $n$:
   493→\[
   494→\sum_{|u|=n}\mu(u)=1.
   495→\]
   496→Consequently,
   497→\[
   498→\sum_{u\in\mathcal I^*}\mu(u)=\infty,
   499→\]
   500→so the prefix weights do not define a probability measure on the whole free
   501→monoid $\mathcal I^*$.  The theory uses stationary aggregation schemes.
   502→
   503→Two canonical average-case aggregations are admitted:
   504→\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\alph*)]
   505→\item a stationary Ces\`aro average;
   506→\item a discounted prefix measure.
   507→\end{enumerate}
   508→
   509→The worst-case aggregation remains the supremum over all finite histories:
   510→\[
   511→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta)
   512→=
   513→\sup_{u\in\mathcal I^*}
   514→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u)).
   515→\]
   516→
   517→The cost profunctor $\mathcal E$ is regime-specific.  Commitment uses a
   518→Boolean or $0/1$ equality cost.  Retention uses KL divergence or a quadratic
   519→Fisher surrogate.  Grounding uses operator-norm distance or a related linear
   520→relaxation cost.  The structural Boolean cost $\{0,\infty\}$ is distinguished
   521→from the normalized operational cost $\{0,1\}$.
   522→
   523→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   524→\subsection{Analytical boundaries}
   525→\label{subsec:boundaries-intro}
   526→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   527→
   528→The framework separates shared syntax from regime-specific analysis.
   529→
   530→\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.0em]
   531→\item \textbf{Shared syntax versus regime-specific semantics.}
   532→The schema provides histories, right congruences, finite quotients, and
   533→variational gaps.  It does not derive the Myhill--Nerode theorem, the
   534→Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem, or the information-bottleneck identity from
   535→categorical axioms alone.
   536→
   537→\item \textbf{Exact theorems versus local theorems.}
   538→The commitment zero-error threshold, the full-KL finite-state
   539→information-bottleneck identity, and the unrestricted linear Hankel spectral
   540→converse are exact within their domains.  The full-KL spectral lower bound is
   541→local and requires explicit regularity assumptions.  The Hankel-structured
   542→finite-rank realization gap satisfies the unrestricted singular-value lower
   543→bound, but equality requires additional structure.
   544→
   545→\item \textbf{Converses versus achievability.}
   546→Spectral converses are lower bounds on approximation gaps.  They become
   547→online regret upper rates only when paired with explicit approximation
   548→achievability bounds.
   549→
   550→\item \textbf{Linear relaxations versus discrete problems.}
   551→The linear Hankel gap and the linear Price-of-Safety surrogate are exact
   552→linear objects.  They do not automatically solve the corresponding discrete
   553→right-congruence optimization problems.
   554→
   555→\item \textbf{Heuristics versus theorems.}
   556→The spectral-tail picture is a heuristic.  The tail shape depends on
   557→aggregation, operator type, affine versus linear approximation, rank versus
   558→state count, compactness, domination, and quadratic-regime assumptions.
   559→
   560→\item \textbf{No unconditional cross-regime ordering.}
   561→The framework does not assert a universal inequality among grounding,
   562→retention, and commitment gaps.  The Independence Theorem shows that the
   563→gaps can be varied independently.
   564→\end{itemize}
   565→
   566→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   567→\subsection{Components of the framework}
   568→\label{subsec:integrated-components-intro}
   569→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   570→
   571→The framework comprises the following components.
   572→
   573→\paragraph{Schatten conditional converse template.}
   574→A task theory with a rank-controlled response operator $A_\delta$ and a
   575→Schatten-$p$ domination inequality satisfies a unified converse:
   576→\[
   577→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
   578→\ge
   579→\frac1{c_p}
   580→\Phi^{(p)}_{r_{\mathbb T}(M)}(A_\delta),
   581→\]
   582→where $r_{\mathbb T}(M)$ is the effective rank budget and
   583→$\Phi^{(p)}_r$ is the Schatten-$p$ tail.
   584→
   585→Grounding is the Schatten-$\infty$ instance for the unrestricted linear
   586→finite-rank relaxation; retention is the Schatten-$1$ instance with effective
   587→rank $M-1$; commitment is the Boolean-rank vertex.  Contextwise $\ell^p$
   588→aggregation does not by itself imply Schatten-$p$ domination.
   589→
   590→\paragraph{Adaptive two-axis oracle inequality.}
   591→Within each type-correct regime, offline approximation and online estimation
   592→compose into a bias--variance decomposition:
   593→\[
   594→\Reg_T(\mathcal A_M)
   595→\le
   596→\Aapp^{\mathsf r}(M)
   597→+
   598→\Est_M^{\mathsf r}(T).
   599→\]
   600→Adaptive aggregation over the state budget $M$ yields an oracle inequality
   601→without knowledge of the optimal budget.  For bounded agnostic losses, the
   602→adaptive penalty is
   603→\[
   604→\PsiAgg_M(T)
   605→=
   606→O\!\left(
   607→\sqrt{T\ln(eM)}
   608→+
   609→\ln(eM)
   610→\right).
   611→\]
   612→Realizable $0/1$ and mixable log-loss model selection have logarithmic
   613→overhead.  Spectral tails enter online rates only under explicit spectral-rate
   614→assumptions.
   615→
   616→Estimation rates may be supplied by a Littlestone bound, a multiclass
   617→Littlestone bound, a mixability bound, or a linear-regret bound.  The oracle
   618→inequality does not assume a single estimation rate across regimes.
   619→
   620→\paragraph{Linear Price-of-Safety surrogate.}
   621→For a quadratic/Fisher retention embedding with state-indexed centered Gram
   622→matrix
   623→\[
   624→G_{st}
   625→=
   626→\sqrt{\pi_s\pi_t}\,
   627→\langle y_s-\bar y,\;y_t-\bar y\rangle,
   628→\]
   629→and a safety partition inducing a pinching conditional expectation
   630→$\EA$, the linear block-local Price-of-Safety surrogate is
   631→\[
   632→\PoSlin(M)
   633→=
   634→\frac12\KyF{G}{M-1}
   635→-
   636→\frac12\KyF{\EA G}{M-1}.
   637→\]
   638→Pinching majorization gives nonnegativity:
   639→\[
   640→\PoSlin(M)\ge0.
   641→\]
   642→The coupling bound
   643→\[
   644→\PoSlin(M)
   645→\le
   646→\frac12\KyF{G-\EA G}{M-1}
   647→\]
   648→is an upper bound, not an equality.  This surrogate is exact for the linear
   649→block-local problem but is not the discrete right-congruence Price of Safety
   650→without relaxation-error control.
   651→
   652→\paragraph{Exponent vertex correspondence.}
   653→The manuscript separates three exponent-like layers:
   654→\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\arabic*)]
   655→\item R\'enyi order $\alpha$;
   656→\item Schatten exponent $p$;
   657→\item aggregation or vertex label.
   658→\end{enumerate}
   659→
   660→Retention is the $\alpha=1$ KL vertex.  Commitment is recovered from the
   661→$\alpha=0$ support vertex after symmetrization and Boolean valuation.
   662→Grounding is organized by the $\alpha=\infty$ supremum vertex, but the exact
   663→unrestricted linear finite-rank grounding cost is operator-norm distance, not
   664→max-divergence.
   665→
   666→The equality of vertex labels
   667→\[
   668→\alpha=p\in\{0,1,\infty\}
   669→\]
   670→is a formal consistency, not a derivation of the Mirsky converses from
   671→R\'enyi identities.
   672→
   673→\paragraph{Temporal protocols.}
   674→On the deterministic Mealy classes $\mathcal H_M$, the reset-word,
   675→persistent-stream, and active residual-identification protocols each have
   676→mistake complexity $\Theta(M\log M)$; the persistent-stream and active
   677→lower bounds are explicit forcing constructions rather than Littlestone
   678→transfers, and agnostic regret is $\Theta(\sqrt{TM\log M})$.

   710→\section{The Schema of Finite-State Approximation}
   711→\label{sec:schema}
   712→%======================================================================
   713→
   714→This section develops the shared finite-state schema.  The schema supplies a
   715→common syntax for all regimes: histories, enriched cost categories, finite
   716→right congruences, quotient machines, stationary aggregation, and profinite
   717→completion.  The analytical meaning of the residual objects is supplied later
   718→by each regime.

   720→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   721→\subsection{The Enriching Poset}
   722→\label{subsec:enriching-poset}
   723→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   724→
   725→All costs take values in the monoidal poset
   726→\[
   727→\mathbf V=([0,\infty],\ge,+,0).
   728→\]
   729→The unit is $0$, and the order is reversed from the usual real order: a
   730→morphism $v\to v'$ exists iff $v\ge v'$ in the usual order.  Thus $0$ is
   731→terminal and $\infty$ is initial.  A morphism $v\to v'$ records that the cost
   732→has decreased from $v$ to $v'$.
   733→
   734→The Boolean sub-poset is
   735→\[
   736→\mathbf V_{\mathbb B}=\{0,\infty\}\subset[0,\infty].
   737→\]
   738→It corresponds to exact equality versus definite mismatch.  A normalized
   739→Boolean mismatch cost with values $\{0,1\}$ is treated separately as an
   740→operational corollary.  The structural zero-error theory uses
   741→$\{0,\infty\}$; the normalized decision-theoretic version uses $\{0,1\}$.
   742→
   743→The reversed-order convention is the standard Lawvere metric convention
   744→\cite{lawvere1973}.
   745→
   746→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   747→\subsection{$\mathbf V$-Categories and $\mathbf V$-Profunctors}
   748→\label{subsec:V-cats-profunctors}
   749→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   750→
   751→A \textbf{$\mathbf V$-category} $\mathbf R$ consists of a set of objects
   752→$\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)$ and hom-objects
   753→\[
   754→\mathbf R(X,Y)\in\mathbf V
   755→\]
   756→satisfying:
   757→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
   758→\item \textbf{Identity.}
   759→\[
   760→0\ge \mathbf R(X,X),
   761→\]
   762→hence $\mathbf R(X,X)=0$.
   763→
   764→\item \textbf{Composition.}
   765→\[
   766→\mathbf R(X,Y)+\mathbf R(Y,Z)\ge \mathbf R(X,Z).
   767→\]
   768→\end{enumerate}
   769→
   770→A \textbf{$\mathbf V$-profunctor}
   771→\[
   772→\mathcal E:\mathbf R^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathbf R\to\mathbf V
   773→\]
   774→assigns costs $\mathcal E(X,Y)$ with left and right actions satisfying
   775→\[
   776→\mathbf R(X',X)+\mathcal E(X,Y)\ge \mathcal E(X',Y),
   777→\]
   778→and
   779→\[
   780→\mathcal E(X,Y)+\mathbf R(Y,Y')\ge \mathcal E(X,Y').
   781→\]
   782→
   783→The profunctor $\mathcal E$ is the cost bimodule.  It measures the mismatch
   784→between residual objects $X$ and $Y$, while the left and right actions express
   785→compatibility with the internal geometry of $\mathbf R$.
   786→
   787→\begin{remark}[Discrete versus enriched residuals]
   788→\label{rem:discrete-vs-enriched}
   789→For retention and commitment, $\mathbf R$ is usually a discrete
   790→$\mathbf V$-category:
   791→\[
   792→\mathbf R(X,Y)=
   793→\begin{cases}
   794→0,&X=Y,\\
   795→\infty,&X\neq Y.
   796→\end{cases}
   797→\]
   798→Then the action inequalities impose no triangle inequality on $\mathcal E$.
   799→KL divergence is not a metric, and Boolean mismatch is not a metric.
   800→
   801→For grounding, $\mathbf R$ may be a metric $\mathbf V$-category of operators,
   802→with
   803→\[
   804→\mathbf R(X,Y)=
   805→\norm{X-Y}_{\mathrm{op}}.
   806→\]
   807→If $\mathcal E$ is the identity profunctor,
   808→\[
   809→\mathcal E(X,Y)=\mathbf R(X,Y),
   810→\]
   811→then the action inequalities reduce to the triangle inequality for the
   812→operator norm.
   813→\end{remark}
   814→
   815→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   816→\subsection{The History $\mathbf V$-Category}
   817→\label{subsec:history-category}
   818→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   819→
   820→Let $\mathcal I$ be a finite input alphabet.  The \textbf{history
   821→$\mathbf V$-category} $\mathbf H$ is the discrete $\mathbf V$-category on the
   822→free monoid $\mathcal I^*$:
   823→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
   824→\item objects are finite words $u\in\mathcal I^*$;
   825→\item hom-objects are
   826→\[
   827→\mathbf H(u,v)=
   828→\begin{cases}
   829→0,&u=v,\\
   830→\infty,&u\neq v.
   831→\end{cases}
   832→\]
   833→\end{itemize}
   834→
   835→Because $\mathbf H$ is discrete, any object-level map
   836→\[
   837→\delta:\mathcal I^*\to\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)
   838→\]
   839→is automatically a $\mathbf V$-functor.  No nontrivial morphism inequalities
   840→need to be checked.
   841→
   842→The prefix structure of histories is not encoded in the hom-objects of
   843→$\mathbf H$.  It is encoded externally in the right-congruence condition,
   844→which ensures that deterministic finite-state approximations evolve
   845→consistently under input extensions.
   846→
   847→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   848→\subsection{Task Theory with Stationary Aggregation}
   849→\label{subsec:task-theory}
   850→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   851→
   852→\begin{definition}[Task Theory]
   853→\label{def:task-theory}
   854→A \textbf{task theory} is a tuple
   855→\[
   856→\mathbb T=(\mathbf H,\mathbf R,\mathcal E,\delta,\mathrm{Agg},\mathcal L)
   857→\]
   858→where:
   859→\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\arabic*)]
   860→\item $\mathbf H$ is the history $\mathbf V$-category.
   861→\item $\mathbf R$ is a $\mathbf V$-category of residual objects.
   862→\item $\mathcal E:\mathbf R^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathbf R\to\mathbf V$ is a
   863→cost profunctor which is \textbf{reflexive}: $\mathcal E(X,X)=0$ for every
   864→$X\in\mathbf R$.  Reflexivity is not automatic for a $\mathbf V$-profunctor.
   865→In the Lawvere quantale $([0,\infty],\ge,+)$ the constant profunctor
   866→$\mathcal E\equiv1$ on a one-object $\mathbf R$ satisfies every profunctor
   867→axiom, and for it the trajectory cannot be realized at any budget even though
   868→its Myhill--Nerode index is $1$.  Every cost used in this manuscript --- KL divergence,
   869→operator norm, and the $0/1$ commitment loss --- is reflexive, so the axiom
   870→constrains nothing in the instances but is required for the generic statements
   871→of Meta-Theorem~\ref{meta:monotone}.
   872→\item $\delta:\mathbf H\to\mathbf R$ is the true trajectory.
   873→\item $\mathrm{Agg}$ is an aggregation scheme, one of:
   874→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
   875→\item a stationary Ces\`aro average;
   876→\item a discounted prefix measure; or
   877→\item the worst-case supremum
   878→$\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta)=\sup_{u\in\mathcal I^*}
   879→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u))$.
   880→\end{itemize}
   881→The first two are \emph{average-case} schemes and require the stationary or
   882→discounted datum; the third requires none.  A task theory carrying the
   883→worst-case scheme is called \emph{uniform}.  Results are stated against a
   884→declared scheme, since the Boolean dichotomy in particular takes different
   885→forms under worst-case and average-case aggregation
   886→(Meta-Theorem~\ref{meta:boolean}).
   887→\item $\mathcal L$ is the resulting cost functional.
   888→\end{enumerate}
   889→\end{definition}
   890→
   891→\begin{definition}[Stationary Ces\`aro aggregation]
   892→\label{def:cesaro-agg}
   893→Let $U_t$ denote the stationary past at time $t$.  For a cost process
   894→\[
   895→c_t=\mathcal E(\delta(U_t),\widehat\delta(U_t)),
   896→\]
   897→define
   898→\[
   899→\overline{\mathcal L}_1(\delta,\widehat\delta)
   900→=
   901→\lim_{T\to\infty}
   902→\frac1T
   903→\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
   904→\mathbb E[c_t],
   905→\]
   906→whenever the limit exists.
   907→\end{definition}
   908→
   909→For finite-state stationary ergodic processes and finite-state
   910→approximations, the limit exists under standard ergodicity assumptions.  In
   911→non-ergodic settings, one may replace the limit by a limsup or by ergodic
   912→decomposition.
   913→
   914→\begin{definition}[Discounted prefix aggregation]
   915→\label{def:discounted-agg}
   916→For $0<\gamma<1$, define a probability measure on $\mathcal I^*$ by
   917→\[
   918→\mu_\gamma(u)
   919→=
   920→(1-\gamma)\gamma^{|u|}
   921→\mathbb P(X_{1:|u|}=u).
   922→\]
   923→Then
   924→\[
   925→\mathcal L_1^\gamma(\delta,\widehat\delta)
   926→=
   927→\mathbb E_{u\sim\mu_\gamma}
   928→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u)).
   929→\]
   930→\end{definition}
   931→
   932→The discounted prefix measure is a probability measure because
   933→\[
   934→\sum_{u\in\mathcal I^*}\mu_\gamma(u)
   935→=
   936→(1-\gamma)
   937→\sum_{n=0}^\infty
   938→\gamma^n
   939→\sum_{|u|=n}
   940→\mathbb P(X_{1:n}=u)
   941→=
   942→(1-\gamma)
   943→\sum_{n=0}^\infty
   944→\gamma^n
   945→=
   946→1.
   947→\]
   948→
   949→The worst-case aggregation remains
   950→\[
   951→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta)
   952→=
   953→\sup_{u\in\mathcal I^*}
   954→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u)).
   955→\]
   956→It is not an average-case aggregation and should not be conflated with the
   957→stationary Ces\`aro or discounted prefix schemes.
   958→
   959→\begin{remark}[Finite Prefixes versus Stationary States]
   960→\label{rem:prefixes-versus-states}
   961→Finite prefixes do not define a probability measure on all of
   962→$\mathcal I^*$.  For stationary retention, the canonical object is the
   963→stationary state process $(S_t)$ and its stationary support $\Splus$.  Finite
   964→histories enter directly in synchronized machines, where $S_t$ is a function
   965→of a finite suffix of the input past.
   966→\end{remark}

   968→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   969→\subsection{Regime-Typed History Actions}
   970→\label{subsec:history-actions}
   971→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   972→
   973→The finite-state resource shared by the three regimes is the index of a finite
   974→quotient of a history system.  Which history system is regime-dependent: the
   975→regimes share the \emph{form} of a finite-index right-action quotient, not a
   976→single literal free monoid.  Forcing every regime onto $\mathcal I^*$ would
   977→misdescribe predictive machines whose state update reads the realized output.
   978→
   979→\begin{definition}[History System]
   980→\label{def:history-system}
   981→A \textbf{history system} is a tuple
   982→\[
   983→\mathsf H=(H,h_\varnothing,\mathcal A,\cdot),
   984→\]
   985→where $H$ is a set of finite histories with root $h_\varnothing$, $\mathcal A$
   986→is an event alphabet, and $\cdot$ is a partial right action
   987→\[
   988→H\times\mathcal A\rightharpoonup H,
   989→\qquad
   990→(h,a)\mapsto h\cdot a,
   991→\]
   992→defined exactly when $a$ is \emph{feasible} from $h$; write
   993→$\mathrm{Feas}(h)\subseteq\mathcal A$ for the set of feasible events.  The
   994→action is \emph{pruned}: whenever $h\cdot a$ is defined it lies in $H$.  If
   995→$\mathrm{Feas}(h)=\mathcal A$ for every $h$, the action is \emph{total}.
   996→\end{definition}
   997→
   998→\begin{definition}[Support-Relative Right Congruence]
   999→\label{def:support-right-cong}
  1000→Let $\mathsf H=(H,h_\varnothing,\mathcal A,\cdot)$ be a history system.  A
  1001→\textbf{support-relative right congruence} on $\mathsf H$ is an equivalence
  1002→relation $\sim$ on $H$ such that $u\sim v$ implies
  1003→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
  1004→\item $\mathrm{Feas}(u)=\mathrm{Feas}(v)$; and
  1005→\item $u\cdot a\sim v\cdot a$ for every $a\in\mathrm{Feas}(u)$.
  1006→\end{enumerate}
  1007→Its \textbf{index} is the number of equivalence classes.  The quotient
  1008→$H/\!\sim$ carries a well-defined partial transition $[u]\cdot a=[u\cdot a]$,
  1009→with $\mathrm{Feas}([u])=\mathrm{Feas}(u)$; clause~(i) is exactly what makes
  1010→the feasible-event set of a class well defined.  For a total action clause~(i)
  1011→is vacuous and the definition reduces to Definition~\ref{def:right-cong}.
  1012→\end{definition}
  1013→
  1014→\begin{remark}[Clause~(i) Is Not Redundant]
  1015→\label{rem:support-clause-i-needed}
  1016→Clause~(ii) alone, read as a condition on commonly feasible events, does not
  1017→give a quotient history system.  Take $\mathcal A=\{a,b\}$ and two histories
  1018→$u,v$ with $\mathrm{Feas}(u)=\{a\}$ and $\mathrm{Feas}(v)=\{b\}$.  The
  1019→relation identifying $u$ with $v$ satisfies clause~(ii) vacuously, there being
  1020→no commonly feasible event, yet the class $[u]$ has no well-defined feasible
  1021→set and the quotient carries no transition structure.  This is the mechanism
  1022→behind Remark~\ref{rem:unifilar-support-not-automatic} below.
  1023→\end{remark}
  1024→
  1025→\begin{remark}[The Regimes as History Systems]
  1026→\label{rem:regime-history-instances}
  1027→The principal instantiations are the following.
  1028→
  1029→\begin{center}
  1030→\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{3.1cm}l@{}}
  1031→\toprule
  1032→regime & $H$ & $\mathcal A$ & action \\
  1033→\midrule
  1034→commitment & $\mathcal I^*$ & $\mathcal I$ & total \\
  1035→retention, unifilar & feasible joint histories
  1036→& $\mathcal I\times\mathcal O$ & pruned \\
  1037→retention, input-driven & $\mathcal I^*$ & $\mathcal I$ & total \\
  1038→grounding & $\Sigma^*$, $\Sigma=\mathcal I\times\mathcal O$ & $\Sigma$ & total \\
  1039→\bottomrule
  1040→\end{tabular}
  1041→\end{center}
  1042→
  1043→For commitment and grounding the action is total and
  1044→Definition~\ref{def:right-cong} applies verbatim.  For unifilar retention the
  1045→feasible joint histories form a pruned tree under the stationary law, and
  1046→compatibility with the action is understood in the support-relative sense.  The
  1047→input-driven model of Definition~\ref{def:controlled-markov} is the case in
  1048→which the state process factors through the input projection, and the
  1049→input-history syntax is recovered.  The history $\mathbf V$-category of
  1050→Section~\ref{subsec:history-category} is built on whichever $H$ the regime
  1051→supplies; being discrete, it makes every object-level trajectory a
  1052→$\mathbf V$-functor regardless of which system is used.
  1053→\end{remark}
  1054→
  1055→\begin{lemma}[Cofilteredness for Support-Relative Congruences]
  1056→\label{lem:cofiltered-support}
  1057→Let $\sim_1,\sim_2$ be finite-index support-relative right congruences on a
  1058→history system $\mathsf H$.  Then $\sim_1\cap\sim_2$ is one as well, refines
  1059→both, and has index at most
  1060→$\operatorname{index}(\sim_1)\operatorname{index}(\sim_2)$.  The finite
  1061→quotients of $\mathsf H$ therefore form a cofiltered system.
  1062→\end{lemma}
  1063→
  1064→\begin{proof}
  1065→The intersection of equivalence relations is an equivalence relation.  If
  1066→$u\,(\sim_1\cap\sim_2)\,v$ then $\mathrm{Feas}(u)=\mathrm{Feas}(v)$ already
  1067→from $\sim_1$, giving clause~(i); and for $a$ in that common feasible set,
  1068→$u\cdot a\sim_iv\cdot a$ for $i=1,2$, giving clause~(ii).  The map
  1069→$[u]_{\sim_1\cap\sim_2}\mapsto([u]_{\sim_1},[u]_{\sim_2})$ is well defined and
  1070→injective, which is the index bound.  The argument is that of
  1071→Lemma~\ref{lem:cofiltered}, with the feasible-set clause carried along.
  1072→\end{proof}

  1074→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1075→\subsection{Right Congruences and Quotients}
  1076→\label{subsec:right-cong}
  1077→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1078→
  1079→\begin{definition}[Right Congruence]
  1080→\label{def:right-cong}
  1081→A \textbf{right congruence} $\sim$ on $\mathbf H$ is an equivalence relation
  1082→on $\mathcal I^*$ such that
  1083→\[
  1084→u\sim v
  1085→\implies
  1086→uw\sim vw
  1087→\quad
  1088→\forall w\in\mathcal I^*.
  1089→\]
  1090→Equivalently, it suffices to require
  1091→\[
  1092→u\sim v
  1093→\implies
  1094→ux\sim vx
  1095→\quad
  1096→\forall x\in\mathcal I.
  1097→\]
  1098→The \textbf{index} of $\sim$ is the number of equivalence classes.
  1099→\end{definition}
  1100→
  1101→This is the total-action instance of Definition~\ref{def:support-right-cong};
  1102→pruned retention history systems use the support-relative form.
  1103→
  1104→The quotient $\mathbf Q=\mathbf H/\!\sim$ has objects $[u]$ and hom-objects
  1105→\[
  1106→\mathbf Q([u],[v])=
  1107→\begin{cases}
  1108→0,&[u]=[v],\\
  1109→\infty,&\text{otherwise}.
  1110→\end{cases}
  1111→\]
  1112→The quotient map
  1113→\[
  1114→Q:\mathbf H\to\mathbf Q,
  1115→\qquad
  1116→u\mapsto[u],
  1117→\]
  1118→is a $\mathbf V$-functor.  The right-congruence condition ensures that the
  1119→quotient carries a well-defined deterministic transition
  1120→\[
  1121→[u]\cdot x=[ux].
  1122→\]
  1123→
  1124→\begin{definition}[$M$-State Approximation]
  1125→\label{def:M-state-approx}
  1126→An \textbf{$M$-state approximation} of a task theory $\mathbb T$ is a pair
  1127→$(\sim,\Psi)$ where $\sim$ is a right congruence of index at most $M$ and
  1128→\[
  1129→\Psi:\mathbf Q\to\mathbf R
  1130→\]
  1131→is a $\mathbf V$-functor.  The approximate trajectory is
  1132→\[
  1133→\Psi\circ Q:\mathbf H\to\mathbf R.
  1134→\]
  1135→\end{definition}
  1136→
  1137→\begin{definition}[$M$-State Gap]
  1138→\label{def:M-state-gap}
  1139→The \textbf{$M$-state gap} is
  1140→\[
  1141→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
  1142→=
  1143→\inf_{\substack{\sim,\Psi\\ \operatorname{index}(\sim)\le M}}
  1144→\mathcal L(\delta,\Psi\circ Q).
  1145→\]
  1146→\end{definition}
  1147→
  1148→\begin{remark}[Machine type depends on $\mathbf R$]
  1149→\label{rem:realization-types}
  1150→The composite $\Psi\circ Q$ has deterministic state-transition structure, but
  1151→its per-state content depends on $\mathbf R$:
  1152→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  1153→\item \textbf{Commitment:} each state stores a residual function; the
  1154→approximant is a deterministic Mealy BSG.
  1155→\item \textbf{Retention:} each state stores a predictive distribution or
  1156→kernel; the approximant is a unifilar lumped controlled predictor
  1157→(Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-lumpable}), and in the input-driven synchronized
  1158→subclass a deterministic-transition, stochastic-emission machine, in the sense
  1159→of Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-machine} and
  1160→Remark~\ref{rem:unifilar-proper-subclass}.
  1161→\item \textbf{Grounding spectral converse:} each state stores an operator or
  1162→linear coordinate; the approximant is a linear finite-rank realization.
  1163→\end{itemize}
  1164→The shared theorems concern $\operatorname{index}(\sim)$.  The
  1165→regime-specific theorems concern the semantic objects attached to the states.
  1166→\end{remark}
  1167→
  1168→\begin{remark}[Stationary Retention Quotients]
  1169→\label{rem:schema-stationary-retention}
  1170→For stationary controlled unifilar machines, the primary feasible objects are
  1171→unifilar-lumpable quotients
  1172→\[
  1173→\phi:\Splus\to\mathcal K
  1174→\]
  1175→(Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-lumpable}).  In a machine with joint-history
  1176→synchronization depth $L$, each such quotient with block-uniform support
  1177→induces a support-relative right congruence on feasible joint histories of
  1178→length at least $L$ by
  1179→\[
  1180→u\sim_\phi v
  1181→\iff
  1182→\phi(\sigma(u))=\phi(\sigma(v))
  1183→\]
  1184→(Proposition~\ref{prop:unifilar-lumpability}); in the input-driven
  1185→synchronized subclass this projects to the input-history right congruence of
  1186→Proposition~\ref{prop:lumpability}.  Thus the finite-state budget remains an
  1187→index resource, now on regime-typed histories.  For non-synchronized
  1188→stationary machines, finite-index right congruences on finite histories should
  1189→be replaced by finite-state factors of the stationary state process.
  1190→\end{remark}

  1193→\subsection{Profinite Completion}
  1194→\label{subsec:profinite-completion}
  1195→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1196→
  1197→The finite quotients of $\mathbf H$ form a cofiltered system; the
  1198→support-relative analogue, for pruned history systems, is
  1199→Lemma~\ref{lem:cofiltered-support}.  Let $\mathcal C$ be the cofiltered poset
  1200→of finite-index right congruences on $\mathcal I^*$, ordered by refinement:
  1201→\[
  1202→\sim_1\le\sim_2
  1203→\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  1204→\sim_1\text{ refines }\sim_2.
  1205→\]
  1206→
  1207→\begin{lemma}[$\mathcal C$ Is Cofiltered]
  1208→\label{lem:cofiltered}
  1209→If $\sim_1$ and $\sim_2$ are finite-index right congruences on $\mathcal I^*$,
  1210→then so is $\sim_1\cap\sim_2$, and it refines both.
  1211→\end{lemma}
  1212→
  1213→\begin{proof}
  1214→The intersection is an equivalence relation.  It is a right congruence: if
  1215→$u\sim_1v$ and $u\sim_2v$, then for every $w\in\mathcal I^*$ we have
  1216→$uw\sim_1vw$ and $uw\sim_2vw$, hence
  1217→$uw\,(\sim_1\cap\sim_2)\,vw$.  The map
  1218→\[
  1219→[u]_{\sim_1\cap\sim_2}
  1220→\mapsto
  1221→\bigl([u]_{\sim_1},\,[u]_{\sim_2}\bigr)
  1222→\]
  1223→is well defined and injective, so
  1224→\[
  1225→\operatorname{index}(\sim_1\cap\sim_2)
  1226→\le
  1227→\operatorname{index}(\sim_1)\,
  1228→\operatorname{index}(\sim_2)
  1229→<
  1230→\infty.
  1231→\]
  1232→Thus $\sim_1\cap\sim_2\in\mathcal C$ refines both, so $\mathcal C$ is
  1233→cofiltered.
  1234→\end{proof}
  1235→
  1236→\begin{definition}[Profinite completion]
  1237→\label{def:profinite-completion}
  1238→The \textbf{profinite completion} of $\mathbf H$ is
  1239→\[
  1240→\widehat{\mathbf H}
  1241→=
  1242→\varprojlim_{\sim\in\mathcal C}
  1243→\mathbf H/\!\sim.
  1244→\]
  1245→\end{definition}
  1246→
  1247→An object of $\widehat{\mathbf H}$ is a compatible family
  1248→\[
  1249→(q_\sim)_\sim,
  1250→\qquad
  1251→q_\sim\in\mathbf H/\!\sim,
  1252→\]
  1253→such that whenever $\sim_1$ refines $\sim_2$, the projection of
  1254→$q_{\sim_1}$ to $\mathbf H/\!\sim_2$ equals $q_{\sim_2}$.
  1255→
  1256→There is a canonical dense embedding
  1257→\[
  1258→\iota:\mathbf H\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathbf H},
  1259→\qquad
  1260→u\mapsto([u]_\sim)_\sim.
  1261→\]
  1262→Injectivity is not formal: it holds because the free monoid $\mathcal I^*$ is
  1263→\emph{residually finite} with respect to finite-index right congruences.
  1264→Given distinct $u\ne v$, set $N>\max\{|u|,|v|\}$ and let $\sim_N$ identify two
  1265→words when they are equal, or when both have length at least $N$.  This is a
  1266→right congruence: if $w\sim_Nw'$ and $x\in\mathcal I$ then either $w=w'$, whence
  1267→$wx=w'x$, or both $|w|,|w'|\ge N$, whence both $|wx|,|w'x|\ge N$.  It has
  1268→finite index $|\mathcal I^{<N}|+1$, and separates $u$ from $v$.  Hence the
  1269→components of $\iota(u)$ and $\iota(v)$ differ at $\sim_N$.
  1270→
  1271→The same separation argument applies to pruned history systems, once
  1272→feasibility is carried along.
  1273→
  1274→\begin{proposition}[Residual Finiteness of Synchronized Unifilar History Trees]
  1275→\label{prop:unifilar-residually-finite}
  1276→Let a unifilar machine (Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-machine}) have
  1277→joint-history synchronization, so that its feasible histories of length at
  1278→least $L$ form a pruned history system $F$ carrying a state readout
  1279→$\sigma:F\to\Splus$ with $\sigma(u\cdot a)=\tau(\sigma(u),a)$ on feasible
  1280→events.  Then $F$ is residually finite with respect to finite-index
  1281→support-relative right congruences: the canonical map from $F$ into the inverse
  1282→limit of its finite quotients is injective.
  1283→\end{proposition}
  1284→
  1285→\begin{proof}
  1286→Let $u\neq v$ in $F$.  Choose $N>\max\{|u|,|v|\}$ and define $\sim_N$ by
  1287→declaring $w\sim_Nw'$ when either $w=w'$, or $|w|,|w'|\ge N$ and
  1288→$\sigma(w)=\sigma(w')$.
  1289→
  1290→\emph{Finite index.}  There are at most
  1291→$\sum_{n<N}|\mathcal I\times\mathcal O|^{\,n}$ singleton classes of length
  1292→below $N$ and at most $|\Splus|$ state-labelled classes at length $N$ or
  1293→above.
  1294→
  1295→\emph{Clause~(i).}  Feasibility of $(x,y)$ from a history $w$ means
  1296→$P_{\sigma(w)}^x(\{y\})>0$, so it is a function of $\sigma(w)$ alone.  Two
  1297→histories identified by $\sim_N$ are either equal or have equal $\sigma$, and
  1298→in both cases have the same feasible events.
  1299→
  1300→\emph{Clause~(ii).}  In the identity case $w\cdot a=w'\cdot a$.  Otherwise
  1301→$|w|,|w'|\ge N$ and $\sigma(w)=\sigma(w')$, so for a feasible $(x,y)$,
  1302→\[
  1303→\sigma\bigl(w\cdot(x,y)\bigr)
  1304→=
  1305→\tau(\sigma(w),x,y)
  1306→=
  1307→\tau(\sigma(w'),x,y)
  1308→=
  1309→\sigma\bigl(w'\cdot(x,y)\bigr),
  1310→\]
  1311→and both extensions have length at least $N+1$, hence
  1312→$w\cdot(x,y)\sim_Nw'\cdot(x,y)$.
  1313→
  1314→Since $N$ exceeds $|u|$ and $|v|$, both are singleton classes of $\sim_N$ and
  1315→are therefore separated, so the corresponding components of their images in
  1316→the inverse limit differ.
  1317→\end{proof}
  1318→
  1319→For total-action regimes the completion is that of the free monoid; for
  1320→synchronized unifilar retention it is the completion of the feasible
  1321→joint-history tree, residually finite by
  1322→Proposition~\ref{prop:unifilar-residually-finite}.
  1323→
  1324→The \textbf{profinite uniformity} on $\mathbf H$ is the initial uniformity
  1325→with respect to the projections
  1326→\[
  1327→\mathbf H\to\mathbf H/\!\sim.
  1328→\]
  1329→Equivalently, its basic entourages are
  1330→\[
  1331→U_\sim
  1332→=
  1333→\{(u,v)\in\mathcal I^*\times\mathcal I^*:u\sim v\},
  1334→\]
  1335→where $\sim$ ranges over finite-index right congruences.
  1336→
  1337→\begin{remark}[Cofiltered limits and Lawvere's framework]
  1338→\label{rem:lawvere-limit}
  1339→The enriched poset
  1340→\[
  1341→\mathbf V=([0,\infty],\ge,+,0)
  1342→\]
  1343→is complete symmetric monoidal closed in the sense of Lawvere
  1344→\cite{lawvere1973}.  The relevant enriched cofiltered limits therefore exist.
  1345→Because $\mathbf V$ is posetal, the enriched inverse limit coincides with the
  1346→ordinary compatible-family limit on objects, equipped with the induced
  1347→profinite uniformity.
  1348→\end{remark}
  1349→
  1350→\begin{remark}[Profinite uniformity for discrete residual categories]
  1351→\label{rem:profinite-discrete}
  1352→Let $\mathbf R$ be a discrete $\mathbf V$-category.  Uniform continuity of a
  1353→trajectory
  1354→\[
  1355→\delta:\mathbf H\to\mathbf R
  1356→\]
  1357→in the profinite uniformity is equivalent to $\delta$ factoring through some
  1358→finite quotient:
  1359→\[
  1360→\mathbf H
  1361→\longrightarrow
  1362→\mathbf H/\!\sim
  1363→\longrightarrow
  1364→\mathbf R
  1365→\]
  1366→for some finite-index right congruence $\sim$.  Indeed, in a discrete
  1367→$\mathbf V$-category the only entourages are those containing the diagonal, so
  1368→uniform continuity requires a finite-index congruence $\sim$ with
  1369→$u\sim v\Rightarrow\delta(u)=\delta(v)$, which is exactly factorization through
  1370→$\mathbf H/\!\sim$.
  1371→\end{remark}
  1372→
  1373→\begin{remark}[Compactness and convergence]
  1374→\label{rem:profinite-compact}
  1375→Each finite quotient $\mathbf H/\!\sim$ is a finite discrete space.  The
  1376→inverse limit $\widehat{\mathbf H}$ is compact in the profinite topology.
  1377→This compactness underlies the profinite convergence meta-theorem: under
  1378→compact-uniform hypotheses on $\mathbf R$ and $\mathcal E$, uniform continuity
  1379→of $\delta$ is equivalent to continuous extension to $\widehat{\mathbf H}$,
  1380→and hence to approximability by finite-index right congruences with vanishing
  1381→gap.
  1382→\end{remark}
  1383→
  1384→The profinite completion is not an analytic Hilbert-space completion such as
  1385→an $L^2$ completion.  It is a logical or Stone-type completion generated by
  1386→finite deterministic quotients.

  1401→\subsection{Separated Task Theories}
  1402→\label{subsec:separated}
  1403→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1404→
  1405→\begin{definition}[Separated Task Theory]
  1406→\label{def:separated-task}
  1407→A task theory $\mathbb T$ is \textbf{separated} if:
  1408→\[
  1409→\mathbf R(X,Y)=\mathbf R(Y,X)=0
  1410→\implies
  1411→X=Y,
  1412→\]
  1413→and
  1414→\[
  1415→\mathcal E(X,Y)=0
  1416→\implies
  1417→X=Y.
  1418→\]
  1419→\end{definition}
  1420→
  1421→\begin{definition}[Myhill--Nerode Equivalence]
  1422→\label{def:nerode}
  1423→For any task theory $\mathbb T$, the \textbf{Myhill--Nerode equivalence}
  1424→$\sim_\delta$ is
  1425→\[
  1426→u\sim_\delta v
  1427→\iff
  1428→\forall w\in\mathcal I^*,
  1429→\quad
  1430→\mathbf R(\delta(uw),\delta(vw))
  1431→=
  1432→\mathbf R(\delta(vw),\delta(uw))
  1433→=
  1434→0.
  1435→\]
  1436→\end{definition}
  1437→
  1438→The two-sided condition is used because $\mathbf R$ need not be symmetric.  If
  1439→$\mathbf R$ is symmetric, the condition reduces to
  1440→\[
  1441→\mathbf R(\delta(uw),\delta(vw))=0
  1442→\quad
  1443→\forall w.
  1444→\]
  1445→
  1446→The Myhill--Nerode equivalence is always a right congruence.  Indeed, if
  1447→$u\sim_\delta v$, then for every $x\in\mathcal I$ and every
  1448→$w\in\mathcal I^*$,
  1449→\[
  1450→\delta(uxw)=\delta(u(xw)),
  1451→\qquad
  1452→\delta(vxw)=\delta(v(xw)),
  1453→\]
  1454→so the defining zero-distance condition for $u$ and $v$ applied to the
  1455→continuation $xw$ gives $ux\sim_\delta vx$.
  1456→
  1457→In the commitment regime, $\sim_\delta$ is the usual Myhill--Nerode residual
  1458→equivalence.  In the retention regime, when predictive distributions are
  1459→distinct on the stationary support, it coincides with causal-state
  1460→equivalence.  In grounding regimes, it may correspond to equality of residual
  1461→kernels or impulse-response functionals, depending on the chosen residual
  1462→category.

  1465→\subsection{Compact Uniform Task Theories}
  1466→\label{subsec:compact-uniform}
  1467→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1468→
  1469→\begin{definition}[Compact Metrizable $\mathbf V$-Category]
  1470→\label{def:compact-metrizable}
  1471→A $\mathbf V$-category $\mathbf R$ is \textbf{compact metrizable} if
  1472→$\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)$ carries a compact metric topology and the
  1473→hom-object map
  1474→\[
  1475→\mathbf R(-,-):
  1476→\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)\times\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)
  1477→\to
  1478→[0,\infty]
  1479→\]
  1480→is continuous in the metric topology, with the usual extended-real topology on
  1481→$[0,\infty]$.
  1482→\end{definition}
  1483→
  1484→More generally, one may use a compact uniform space in place of a compact
  1485→metric space.  The profinite convergence meta-theorem is stated under the
  1486→hypothesis that $\mathbf R$ is compact metrizable or compact uniform and that
  1487→the cost profunctor $\mathcal E$ is uniformly continuous and separated.
  1488→
  1489→\begin{remark}[Separate Continuity Is Insufficient]
  1490→\label{rem:separate-continuity-insufficient}
  1491→Separate continuity alone is insufficient for the profinite equivalence.  The
  1492→meta-theorem uses joint uniform continuity, or compact-uniform continuity, of
  1493→the cost profunctor.
  1494→\end{remark}

  1497→\subsection{Hilbert-Module Task Theories and Linear Hankel Gaps}
  1498→\label{subsec:hilbert-module}
  1499→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1500→
  1501→\begin{definition}[Hilbert-Module Task Theory]
  1502→\label{def:hilbert-module}
  1503→A \textbf{Hilbert-module task theory} is a separated task theory on a finite
  1504→joint alphabet
  1505→\[
  1506→\Sigma=\mathcal I\times\mathcal O
  1507→\]
  1508→equipped with the following additional data:
  1509→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  1510→\item a Banach space $B$ of residual observables;
  1511→\item a bounded linear functional
  1512→\[
  1513→\phi:B\to\mathbb C;
  1514→\]
  1515→\item an impulse-response function
  1516→\[
  1517→h:\Sigma^*\to\mathbb C
  1518→\]
  1519→obtained from the true trajectory by
  1520→\[
  1521→h(u)=\phi(\delta(u));
  1522→\]
  1523→\item a Hankel operator
  1524→\[
  1525→H_\delta:\ell^2(\Sigma^*)\to\ell^2(\Sigma^*)
  1526→\]
  1527→specified on the standard basis by
  1528→\[
  1529→(H_\delta e_u)(v)=h(uv),
  1530→\]
  1531→\emph{provided} this densely defined formula extends to a bounded operator on
  1532→$\ell^2(\Sigma^*)$.  Boundedness is not automatic: for general $h$ the vector
  1533→$v\mapsto h(uv)$ need not be square summable, and even when it is, the induced
  1534→matrix need not be bounded.  A sufficient condition is the geometric decay
  1535→hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:stable-decay}, which yields boundedness by
  1536→the Schur test;
  1537→\item compactness of the resulting bounded operator $H_\delta$.
  1538→\end{enumerate}
  1539→A Hilbert-module task theory is said to be \emph{spectrally admissible} when
  1540→the Hankel operator exists as a bounded operator and is compact, i.e.\ when
  1541→conditions~(iv) and~(v) hold.  All singular-value statements below are
  1542→asserted only for spectrally admissible task theories.
  1543→\end{definition}
  1544→
  1545→If the residual objects are operators, $B$ may be an operator space or a
  1546→$C^*$-algebra, and $\phi$ may be a bounded functional selecting the scalar
  1547→observable relevant to the impulse response.  The scalar impulse response $h$
  1548→is the object seen by the Hankel operator.  Distinct residual objects may
  1549→induce the same impulse response; the Hankel theory below depends only on $h$
  1550→unless additional domination hypotheses are supplied.
  1551→
  1552→\begin{remark}[Residual Norm versus Hankel Norm]
  1553→\label{rem:hilbert-residual-hankel}
  1554→The norm used for residual-operator comparisons is the norm of $B$, while the
  1555→Hankel gap uses the $\ell^2$ operator norm of $H_\delta$.  No domination
  1556→between the $B$-norm and the Hankel operator norm is assumed.
  1557→\end{remark}
  1558→
  1559→The model does not assume an unqualified domination hypothesis of the form
  1560→\[
  1561→\norm{H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1562→\le
  1563→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta).
  1564→\]
  1565→Such an inequality may hold under explicit decay or summability conditions,
  1566→but it is not a general axiom.
  1567→
  1568→\begin{definition}[Unrestricted Linear Hankel Relaxation Gap]
  1569→\label{def:linear-hankel-gap}
  1570→The \textbf{unrestricted linear Hankel $M$-rank relaxation gap} is
  1571→\[
  1572→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)
  1573→=
  1574→\inf_{\operatorname{rank}\widehat H\le M}
  1575→\norm{H_\delta-\widehat H}_{\mathrm{op}}.
  1576→\]
  1577→The infimum is over bounded operators $\widehat H$ of rank at most $M$, not
  1578→necessarily Hankel operators.
  1579→\end{definition}
  1580→
  1581→This is an operator-approximation gap.  It is the object to which
  1582→Eckart--Young--Mirsky \cite{eckart1936,mirsky1960} applies directly.
  1583→
  1584→\begin{definition}[Hankel-Structured Finite-Rank Gap]
  1585→\label{def:hankel-structured-gap}
  1586→The \textbf{Hankel-structured finite-rank gap} is
  1587→\[
  1588→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{Hank,str}}(M)
  1589→=
  1590→\inf_{\substack{
  1591→\operatorname{rank}\widehat H\le M\\
  1592→\widehat H\ \text{Hankel}
  1593→}}
  1594→\norm{H_\delta-\widehat H}_{\mathrm{op}}.
  1595→\]
  1596→The infimum is over Hankel operators of rank at most $M$.
  1597→\end{definition}
  1598→
  1599→Because the Hankel-structured feasible set is a subset of the unrestricted
  1600→feasible set,
  1601→\[
  1602→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{Hank,str}}(M)
  1603→\ge
  1604→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M).
  1605→\]
  1606→Equality requires additional structure.
  1607→
  1608→\begin{definition}[Linear Finite-Rank Residual-Cost Gap]
  1609→\label{def:lin-finite-rank-gap}
  1610→The \textbf{linear finite-rank residual-cost gap} is
  1611→\[
  1612→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{lin}}(M)
  1613→=
  1614→\inf
  1615→\left\{
  1616→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta):
  1617→\operatorname{rank}H_{\widehat\delta}\le M
  1618→\right\}.
  1619→\]
  1620→\end{definition}
  1621→
  1622→The residual-cost gap $\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{lin}}(M)$ and the
  1623→unrestricted Hankel relaxation gap
  1624→$\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)$ are distinct objects.  The former
  1625→measures task loss of finite-rank residual approximants; the latter measures
  1626→operator-norm approximation to $H_\delta$.  They are related only under
  1627→explicit domination or achievability hypotheses.
  1628→
  1629→\begin{definition}[Weighted Residual Norm]
  1630→\label{def:weighted-residual-norm}
  1631→For $0<\rho<1$, define
  1632→\[
  1633→\norm{d}_{\infty,\rho}
  1634→=
  1635→\sup_{w\in\Sigma^*}
  1636→\rho^{-|w|}
  1637→|d(w)|.
  1638→\]
  1639→\end{definition}
  1640→
  1641→\begin{proposition}[Stable-Decay Hankel Domination]
  1642→\label{prop:stable-decay}
  1643→Suppose
  1644→\[
  1645→|h_\delta(w)-h_{\widehat\delta}(w)|
  1646→\le
  1647→\varepsilon\rho^{|w|}
  1648→\]
  1649→with
  1650→\[
  1651→0<\rho<\frac1{|\Sigma|}.
  1652→\]
  1653→Then
  1654→\[
  1655→\norm{H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1656→\le
  1657→\frac{1}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}
  1658→\varepsilon.
  1659→\]
  1660→Equivalently, if
  1661→\[
  1662→\norm{h_\delta-h_{\widehat\delta}}_{\infty,\rho}
  1663→\le
  1664→\varepsilon,
  1665→\]
  1666→then
  1667→\[
  1668→\norm{H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1669→\le
  1670→\frac{1}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}
  1671→\norm{h_\delta-h_{\widehat\delta}}_{\infty,\rho}.
  1672→\]
  1673→\end{proposition}
  1674→
  1675→\begin{proof}
  1676→Let
  1677→\[
  1678→A=H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}.
  1679→\]
  1680→The Hankel matrix entries satisfy
  1681→\[
  1682→|A(u,v)|
  1683→=
  1684→|h_\delta(uv)-h_{\widehat\delta}(uv)|
  1685→\le
  1686→\varepsilon\rho^{|u|+|v|}.
  1687→\]
  1688→By Schur's test,
  1689→\[
  1690→\sup_u\sum_v |A(u,v)|
  1691→\le
  1692→\varepsilon
  1693→\sup_u
  1694→\rho^{|u|}
  1695→\sum_{v\in\Sigma^*}
  1696→\rho^{|v|}.
  1697→\]
  1698→Since $\rho^{|u|}\le1$ and the number of words of length $n$ is
  1699→$|\Sigma|^n$,
  1700→\[
  1701→\sum_{v\in\Sigma^*}\rho^{|v|}
  1702→=
  1703→\sum_{n=0}^\infty
  1704→|\Sigma|^n\rho^n
  1705→=
  1706→\frac{1}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}.
  1707→\]
  1708→Thus
  1709→\[
  1710→\sup_u\sum_v |A(u,v)|
  1711→\le
  1712→\frac{\varepsilon}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}.
  1713→\]
  1714→The same bound holds for columns.  Schur's test therefore gives
  1715→\[
  1716→\norm{A}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1717→\le
  1718→\frac{\varepsilon}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}.
  1719→\]
  1720→\end{proof}
  1721→
  1722→\begin{remark}[AAK versus EYM]
  1723→\label{rem:aak-eym-hilbert}
  1724→The default spectral converse for
  1725→$\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)$ is Eckart--Young--Mirsky:
  1726→\[
  1727→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)
  1728→=
  1729→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\delta).
  1730→\]
  1731→Adamjan--Arov--Krein applies only after a Hardy-space embedding has been
  1732→separately established.  The model does not assume such an embedding by
  1733→default.
  1734→\end{remark}

  1736→\begin{remark}[Infinite support and boundedness]
  1737→\label{rem:infinite-support}
  1738→Infinite support of an impulse response does not by itself imply that the
  1739→associated Hankel operator is unbounded.  Boundedness requires decay or
  1740→summability conditions.  For example, scalar impulse responses satisfying
  1741→$|h(u)|\le C\rho^{|u|}$ with $0<\rho<1/|\Sigma|$ define bounded Hankel-type
  1742→operators by the Schur-test argument above.  Bounded symbolic impulse
  1743→responses that fail to decay may or may not define bounded Hankel operators;
  1744→boundedness is checked case by case.
  1745→\end{remark}

  1748→\subsection{Stationary Controlled Causal Machines}
  1749→\label{subsec:controlled-markov}
  1750→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1751→
  1752→\begin{definition}[Stationary Controlled Causal Machine]
  1753→\label{def:controlled-markov}
  1754→A \textbf{stationary controlled causal machine} consists of:
  1755→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  1756→\item a finite input alphabet $\mathcal I$;
  1757→\item a standard Borel output space $\mathcal O$;
  1758→\item a finite state set $\mathcal S$;
  1759→\item a deterministic transition map
  1760→\[
  1761→\tau:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal S;
  1762→\]
  1763→\item emission laws
  1764→\[
  1765→P_s\in\mathcal P(\mathcal O)
  1766→\]
  1767→for each $s\in\mathcal S$;
  1768→\item a stationary ergodic input process $(X_t)_{t\in\mathbb Z}$;
  1769→\item a stationary state process $(S_t)_{t\in\mathbb Z}$ satisfying
  1770→\[
  1771→S_t=\tau(S_{t-1},X_t)
  1772→\quad\text{a.s.};
  1773→\]
  1774→\item a stationary distribution
  1775→\[
  1776→\pi_s=\mathbb P(S_t=s);
  1777→\]
  1778→\item a predictive future variable $Z_t$, by default the next output $Y_t$,
  1779→such that $S_t$ is sufficient:
  1780→\[
  1781→Z_t\perp\!\!\!\perp
  1782→\{S_u,X_u,Y_u:u<t\}
  1783→\mid S_t.
  1784→\]
  1785→\end{enumerate}
  1786→The predictive distribution of state $s$ is
  1787→\[
  1788→P_s=\mathcal L(Z_t\mid S_t=s).
  1789→\]
  1790→The stationary support is
  1791→\[
  1792→\Splus=\{s\in\mathcal S:\pi_s>0\}.
  1793→\]
  1794→\end{definition}
  1795→
  1796→\begin{definition}[Stationary Controlled Unifilar Causal Machine]
  1797→\label{def:unifilar-machine}
  1798→A \textbf{stationary controlled unifilar causal machine} consists of a finite
  1799→input alphabet $\mathcal I$, a standard Borel output space $\mathcal O$, a
  1800→finite state set $\mathcal S$, emission kernels
  1801→\[
  1802→P_s^x=\mathcal L\bigl(Y_t\mid S_t=s,\ X_t=x\bigr),
  1803→\]
  1804→a measurable \emph{unifilar} update
  1805→\[
  1806→\tau:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\times\mathcal O\to\mathcal S,
  1807→\qquad
  1808→S_{t+1}=\tau(S_t,X_t,Y_t)\quad\text{a.s.},
  1809→\]
  1810→defined on jointly feasible triples, together with a stationary ergodic input
  1811→process, a stationary distribution $\pi$, and a declared predictive target
  1812→$Z_t$.
  1813→
  1814→An update with $\tau(s,x,y)=\tau_0(s,x)$ for every feasible $y$ is called
  1815→\emph{output-independent}; in that case the recursion collapses to
  1816→$S_{t+1}=\tau_0(S_t,X_t)$ and, after an index shift, the machine is exactly a
  1817→stationary controlled causal machine in the sense of
  1818→Definition~\ref{def:controlled-markov}.
  1819→\end{definition}
  1820→
  1821→\begin{remark}[The Input-Driven Model Is a Proper Subclass]
  1822→\label{rem:unifilar-proper-subclass}
  1823→The containment is strict, and the witness is standard.  Take
  1824→$|\mathcal I|=1$, $\mathcal O=\{0,1\}$, two states $A,B$, with $A$ emitting
  1825→$0$ or $1$ with equal probability and $B$ emitting $1$ almost surely, and
  1826→\[
  1827→\tau(A,\cdot,0)=A,\qquad \tau(A,\cdot,1)=B,\qquad \tau(B,\cdot,1)=A .
  1828→\]
  1829→Since the input alphabet is a singleton, the input history of length $t$ is
  1830→the unique word of that length, yet the state after two steps is $A$ on the
  1831→output history $00$ and $B$ on $01$.  The state is therefore not a function of
  1832→the input history, and no map $\tau_0:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal S$
  1833→reproduces this machine.
  1834→
  1835→Consequently Definition~\ref{def:controlled-markov} describes an
  1836→\emph{input-driven} transducer, and the descriptions of it as a lumped
  1837→$\epsilon$-machine refer to this output-independent subclass rather than to
  1838→general controlled $\epsilon$-machines, whose causal states are functions of
  1839→joint input--output histories
  1840→(Remark~\ref{rem:epsilon-machine-relation}).  The retention theory is developed
  1841→for the full unifilar class in
  1842→Section~\ref{subsec:unifilar-retention}, with the input-driven model as the
  1843→output-independent specialization; the complexity constructions of
  1844→Section~\ref{subsec:retention-complexity} are stated for the input-driven model
  1845→and transfer to the unifilar class by
  1846→Remark~\ref{rem:complexity-transfer}.
  1847→\end{remark}
  1848→
  1849→\begin{definition}[$Z$-predictive equivalence]
  1850→\label{def:z-predictive-equivalence}
  1851→Fix the predictive target $Z_t$.  On the stationary support define
  1852→\[
  1853→s\sim_Z s'
  1854→\iff
  1855→\mathcal L(Z_t\mid S_t=s)
  1856→=
  1857→\mathcal L(Z_t\mid S_t=s').
  1858→\]
  1859→If $Z_t$ is the entire controlled future, $\sim_Z$ is causal-state
  1860→equivalence.  If $Z_t=Y_t$ is only the next output, $\sim_Z$ is one-step
  1861→predictive equivalence, which is strictly coarser in general and need not
  1862→identify causal states.
  1863→
  1864→Throughout, ``causal equivalence'' means $\sim_Z$ for the declared target
  1865→$Z$, and coincides with causal-state equivalence exactly when $Z$ is the full
  1866→future.  All retention statements, including the zero-retention threshold,
  1867→are to be read with respect to distinct $Z$-predictive states.
  1868→
  1869→For unifilar machines with input-dependent emissions
  1870→(Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-machine}) the predictive object attached to a
  1871→state is the whole kernel $x\mapsto P_s^x$, and $\sim_Z$ compares kernels on
  1872→the input support: $s\sim_Zs'$ if and only if $P_s^x=P_{s'}^x$ for every input
  1873→$x$ of positive mass.
  1874→\end{definition}
  1875→
  1876→Finite output alphabets are the special case in which all $P_s$ are supported
  1877→on a finite set.  Gaussian quadratic retention is obtained by taking
  1878→$\mathcal O=\mathbb R^D$ and $P_s$ Gaussian.
  1879→
  1880→\begin{definition}[Synchronized Finite-History Realization]
  1881→\label{def:synchronized-realization}
  1882→A stationary controlled causal machine has \textbf{synchronization depth}
  1883→$L<\infty$ if there exists a surjection
  1884→\[
  1885→\sigma_L:\mathcal I^L\to\Splus
  1886→\]
  1887→such that, almost surely,
  1888→\[
  1889→S_t=\sigma_L(X_{t-L+1},\dots,X_t).
  1890→\]
  1891→For histories $u$ with $|u|\ge L$, define
  1892→\[
  1893→\sigma(u)=\sigma_L(\operatorname{suffix}_L(u)).
  1894→\]
  1895→Then for all sufficiently long $u$,
  1896→\[
  1897→\sigma(ux)=\tau(\sigma(u),x).
  1898→\]
  1899→The finite-history trajectory is
  1900→\[
  1901→\delta(u)=P_{\sigma(u)}.
  1902→\]
  1903→\end{definition}
  1904→
  1905→Thus the original finite-history/right-congruence syntax is recovered for
  1906→definite and synchronizing machines, including all NP-hardness constructions.
  1907→
  1908→\begin{definition}[Causal Equivalence]
  1909→\label{def:causal-equivalence}
  1910→On the stationary support, causal equivalence is
  1911→\[
  1912→s\sim_\delta t
  1913→\iff
  1914→P_s=P_t.
  1915→\]
  1916→In a synchronized machine, the corresponding history equivalence is
  1917→\[
  1918→u\sim_\delta v
  1919→\iff
  1920→\sigma(u)=\sigma(v).
  1921→\]
  1922→If the predictive distributions $P_s$ are pairwise distinct on $\Splus$, then
  1923→causal equivalence coincides with state identity on $\Splus$.
  1924→\end{definition}
  1925→
  1926→\begin{definition}[Lumpable Quotient]
  1927→\label{def:lumpable-quotient}
  1928→A map $\phi:\Splus\to\mathcal K$ is \textbf{lumpable} if there exists a
  1929→deterministic quotient transition
  1930→\[
  1931→\tau_{\mathcal K}:\mathcal K\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal K
  1932→\]
  1933→such that
  1934→\[
  1935→\phi(\tau(s,x))
  1936→=
  1937→\tau_{\mathcal K}(\phi(s),x)
  1938→\]
  1939→for all $s\in\Splus$ and all inputs $x$ in the support of the input process.
  1940→For worst-case aggregation, require this for all $x\in\mathcal I$.
  1941→The quotient process is
  1942→\[
  1943→K_t=\phi(S_t).
  1944→\]
  1945→\end{definition}
  1946→
  1947→\begin{definition}[Unifilar Lumpability]
  1948→\label{def:unifilar-lumpable}
  1949→A quotient $\phi:\mathcal S\to\mathcal K$ of a unifilar machine is
  1950→\textbf{unifilar-lumpable} if there is
  1951→$\tau_{\mathcal K}:\mathcal K\times\mathcal I\times\mathcal O\to\mathcal K$
  1952→with
  1953→\[
  1954→\phi\bigl(\tau(s,x,y)\bigr)=\tau_{\mathcal K}\bigl(\phi(s),x,y\bigr)
  1955→\]
  1956→for every \emph{jointly feasible} triple $(s,x,y)$, that is, every triple with
  1957→$P_s^x(\{y\})>0$ in the discrete case and, in general, every triple in the
  1958→support of the joint law.  The singleton quotient is always
  1959→unifilar-lumpable.
  1960→
  1961→The quotient has \textbf{block-uniform support} if
  1962→\[
  1963→\phi(s)=\phi(s')
  1964→\implies
  1965→\supp P_s^x=\supp P_{s'}^x
  1966→\qquad\text{for every }x
  1967→\]
  1968→in the input support.  More generally, say that $\phi$ has \textbf{connected
  1969→support} if for every block $C$ and every input $x$ the graph on $C$ joining
  1970→$s$ to $s'$ when $\supp P_s^x\cap\supp P_{s'}^x\neq\varnothing$ is connected;
  1971→block-uniform support is the special case in which that graph is complete.
  1972→\end{definition}
  1973→
  1974→\begin{remark}[Unifilar Lumpability Does Not Reduce to
  1975→Definition~\ref{def:lumpable-quotient}]
  1976→\label{rem:unifilar-support-not-automatic}
  1977→For an output-independent update Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-lumpable} is
  1978→strictly weaker than Definition~\ref{def:lumpable-quotient}, and the two do not
  1979→collapse onto each other.  Writing
  1980→$\tau(s,x,y)=\tau_0(s,x)$, unifilar lumpability requires
  1981→$\phi(\tau_0(s,x))=\phi(\tau_0(s',x))$ only for $s,s'$ in a common block that
  1982→share a feasible output $y$ at input $x$; ordinary lumpability requires it for
  1983→\emph{all} $s,s'$ in the block.  When within-block emission supports are
  1984→disjoint the requirement is vacuous and the unifilar notion is strictly weaker.
  1985→
  1986→An explicit witness has $\mathcal I=\{0,1\}$, $\mathcal O=\{0,1\}$, three
  1987→states, and
  1988→\[
  1989→\tau_0(s_0,0)=s_0,\quad \tau_0(s_0,1)=s_1,\qquad
  1990→\tau_0(s_1,0)=s_0,\quad \tau_0(s_1,1)=s_2,
  1991→\]
  1992→\[
  1993→\tau_0(s_2,0)=s_0,\quad \tau_0(s_2,1)=s_0,
  1994→\]
  1995→with $s_0,s_1$ emitting $0$ almost surely and $s_2$ emitting $1$ almost surely.
  1996→The chain is irreducible with strictly positive stationary distribution.  The
  1997→partition $\{\{s_0,s_2\},\{s_1\}\}$ is unifilar-lumpable --- $s_0$ and $s_2$
  1998→never share a feasible output, so no intertwining is demanded of them --- yet
  1999→it is not lumpable, since $\tau_0(s_0,1)=s_1$ and $\tau_0(s_2,1)=s_0$ lie in
  2000→different blocks.
