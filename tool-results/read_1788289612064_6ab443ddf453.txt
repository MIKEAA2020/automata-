   106→\title{
   107→\bfseries
   108→The Rate--Distortion Theory of Bounded Sequential Transduction:\\
   109→A Comparative Syntax for Finite-State Approximation
   110→}
   111→\author{}
   112→\date{}
   113→

   115→\maketitle

   117→%======================================================================
   118→\begin{abstract}
   119→We develop a comparative framework for finite-state approximation of
   120→sequential objects.  The framework uses histories, finite-index right
   121→congruences, state-count resources, enriched cost profunctors, stationary
   122→aggregation, and variational gaps.  Commitment uses deterministic Mealy
   123→machines and Myhill--Nerode residuals.  Retention uses stationary controlled
   124→unifilar causal machines --- controlled $\epsilon$-machines, whose state update
   125→$S_{t+1}=\tau(S_t,X_t,Y_t)$ may read the realized output; the input-driven
   126→synchronized subclass, in which causal states factor through input histories,
   127→contains all complexity constructions.
   128→Grounding uses linear finite-rank Hankel realizations, whose resource is
   129→operator rank rather than quotient index; a closed-form finite-budget
   130→characterization of exact symbolic deterministic grounding remains open, but
   131→an observable deterministic floor, its finite-state limit, and certified
   132→finite-horizon and finite-section intervals are established below.  Task theories use stationary
   133→Ces\`aro aggregation or discounted prefix measures.  Finite prefixes do not
   134→define a probability measure over all lengths.
   135→
   136→The retention full-KL gap satisfies an exact finite-state
   137→information-bottleneck decomposition over stationary lumpable quotients, whose
   138→controlled analogue, conditional on the input, holds for general unifilar
   139→machines (Theorem~\ref{thm:controlled-ib}):
   140→\[
   141→\RetKL(M)
   142→=
   143→I(S;Z)
   144→-
   145→\max_{\substack{
   146→\phi\ \text{lumpable}\\
   147→|\mathcal K|\le M
   148→}}
   149→I(K_\phi;Z).
   150→\]
   151→Zero retention occurs exactly when $M$ is at least the stationary support size
   152→of distinct predictive states.  The Gaussian quadratic restricted gap
   153→satisfies a covariance-tail lower bound with effective rank $M-1$:
   154→\[
   155→\RetQuad(M)
   156→\ge
   157→\frac12
   158→\sum_{i\ge M}
   159→\lambda_i(\Sigma_\pi).
   160→\]
   161→Full KL admits a precise local Fisher asymptotic lower bound under regularity
   162→assumptions.
   163→
   164→The unrestricted linear finite-rank Hankel relaxation gap satisfies
   165→\[
   166→\Dunres(M)
   167→=
   168→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu)
   169→\]
   170→by Eckart--Young--Mirsky \cite{eckart1936,mirsky1960}.  The Hankel-structured finite-rank realization gap
   171→satisfies the lower bound
   172→\[
   173→\DHankstr(M)
   174→\ge
   175→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu),
   176→\]
   177→with equality only under additional structural hypotheses.  For a single-letter
   178→alphabet, $|\Sigma|=1$, the shift-intertwining Hardy-space unitary of
   179→Theorem~\ref{thm:aak-equality} is the canonical basis-to-monomial
   180→correspondence and is therefore automatic, not a separately supplied
   181→hypothesis; equality then follows from Adamjan--Arov--Krein provided the one
   182→remaining hypothesis holds, namely that the transported symbol is compact.
   183→For $|\Sigma|>1$ the free monoid carries no shift of multiplicity one, the
   184→scalar theorem does not apply, and equality remains conditional on a
   185→Nehari/AAK-type finite-rank approximation theorem for the multi-shift
   186→setting, which is not established here.
   187→
   188→For finite output alphabets a global converse holds in probability
   189→coordinates: $\RetKL(M)\ge\sum_{i\ge M}\lambda_i(\Sigma_p)$, where
   190→$\Sigma_p$ is the covariance of the predictive probability vectors, with no
   191→regularity or small-radius hypothesis.  The constant $1$ is optimal, being
   192→approached by antipodal binary perturbations of the uniform law.  Under uniform
   193→interiority a corresponding bound holds in a fixed minimal natural-parameter
   194→chart, with a constant governed by the least eigenvalue of the log-partition
   195→Hessian on that chart.  That interiority hypothesis cannot be removed: in the
   196→natural-parameter chart no universal constant exists, since two Bernoulli laws
   197→approaching opposite faces of the simplex keep the retention cost below
   198→$\log2$ while their parameter covariance diverges.  There is likewise no
   199→canonical global ``Fisher covariance'' independent of the choice of chart.
   200→
   201→The computational-complexity theory gives NP-completeness for Gaussian
   202→quadratic restricted retention already for minimal definite reset machines of
   203→synchronization depth one.  A tagged depth-two construction also gives
   204→NP-hardness.  For unrestricted full-KL retention over a finite output alphabet,
   205→embedding $k$-means into a small interior neighborhood of the uniform law makes
   206→the Jensen--Shannon block cost a controlled perturbation of the Euclidean one
   207→and yields NP-hardness under a promise, again for depth-one reset machines in
   208→which every partition is lumpable.  Because the embedding controls the
   209→objective relatively and not merely across a promise gap, the reduction is
   210→approximation preserving, and the problem is APX-hard: no polynomial-time
   211→approximation scheme exists unless $\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}$.  Both statements
   212→concern an output alphabet whose size grows with the dimension of the embedded
   213→instance; hardness for a fixed alphabet is not established.  The result there is
   214→hardness rather than completeness, since deciding inequalities between
   215→rationals and sums of logarithms of rationals is not known to be polynomial.
   216→
   217→The temporal theory distinguishes three protocols: reset-word prediction,
   218→persistent-stream prediction, and active query learning.  In the reset-word
   219→realizable protocol, standard automata VC/Littlestone estimates give
   220→$\Theta(M\log M)$ mistake complexity over fixed nontrivial alphabets.  For a
   221→single persistent transducer stream, finite-class counting gives the
   222→unconditional upper bound $O(M\log M)$, and an explicit stream-realizable
   223→mistake tree gives the matching $\Omega(M\log M)$ lower bound, so the
   224→persistent-stream complexity is also $\Theta(M\log M)$ for every fixed
   225→alphabet pair with $|\mathcal I|\ge2$, $|\mathcal O|\ge2$.  In the active protocol the learner chooses its own inputs, so mistakes are
   226→counted against an explicit identification objective: $\MistRI(M)$ denotes the
   227→mistakes incurred before the learner determines the Myhill--Nerode residual
   228→class of the current history.  Some such objective is unavoidable, since an
   229→unconstrained active learner may repeat an input of known output forever and
   230→make no mistakes at all.  Against that objective,
   231→\[
   232→\MistRI(M)
   233→\le
   234→O(\Esync(M)),
   235→\]
   236→and on the full class $\mathcal H_M$ this is $\Theta(M\log M)$
   237→\emph{unconditionally}: an active halving learner attains the objective within
   238→$\log_2|\mathcal H_M\times Q|=O(M\log M)$ mistakes, and an explicit gated
   239→family forces $\Omega(M\log M)$, so
   240→\[
   241→\MistRI(M)
   242→=
   243→\Theta(M\log M),
   244→\]
   245→with no synchronizability or operational-equivalence hypothesis.  This is a
   246→statement about active residual identification, not about unrestricted active
   247→prediction.
   248→State identification in a known minimal skeleton costs
   249→$\Theta_{|\mathcal O|}(\log M)$ mistakes --- the halving upper bound is matched
   250→by a cyclic-shift family on which each round exposes one fresh bit of the
   251→initial state --- so a decomposition whose second term is a
   252→state-identification mistake complexity is degenerate for every subclass; a
   253→direct-sum instance does force its two components on disjoint rounds, but both
   254→components realized here are model-identification components, and no matching
   255→upper bound in the two components separately is claimed.
   256→Finite-class aggregation gives agnostic regret $O(\sqrt{T M\log M})$; matching
   257→lower bounds are inherited only in protocols admitting an $\Omega(M\log M)$
   258→sequential shattering construction.  In the active protocol neither bound
   259→follows merely from letting the learner choose inputs: the $\Omega(M\log M)$
   260→lower bound is supplied by the explicit gated construction rather than by
   261→transfer from the passive setting, and separating the two terms of the additive
   262→form still requires a direct-sum instance.
   263→
   264→A conditional Schatten template states that a rank-controlled response
   265→operator together with a Schatten-$p$ domination inequality yields a
   266→Schatten-$p$ tail lower bound.  An oracle inequality composes offline
   267→approximation and online estimation within type-correct hypothesis classes.
   268→A conditional continuous bias--variance sandwich is proved for regular
   269→approximation and estimation envelopes, and, under the explicit approximation
   270→and estimation floors of Assumption~\ref{ass:oracle-floors}, a matching
   271→minimax lower bound is proved, so the envelope is optimal up to the
   272→aggregation penalty.  Whether that penalty is itself necessary remains open.
   273→
   274→A linear second-order safety surrogate based on pinching satisfies Ky Fan
   275→majorization and is distinct from the discrete right-congruence Price of
   276→Safety.  A vertex correspondence labels retention by $\alpha=1$, commitment by
   277→a Booleanized symmetrized $\alpha=0$ support vertex, and grounding by an
   278→$\alpha=\infty$ operator-norm face; this is an organizing analogy, recorded as
   279→a formal consistency rather than a derivation, and none of the analytic
   280→theorems depend on it.  Every approximation claim carries an explicit type
   281→signature fixing regime, feasible set, aggregation, support, protocol, and
   282→analytical bridge, so that statements from different regimes are not
   283→conflated.  The framework separates shared finite-state syntax from regime-specific
   284→analysis: a typed variational schema, structural monotonicity and zero
   285→thresholds, a conditional response-operator converse, and regime-specific
   286→analytical engines.  It identifies a small set of sharply posed open research
   287→programs in place of a single undifferentiated open problem of exact
   288→symbolic deterministic grounding.
   289→\end{abstract}

   293→\section{Introduction: The Bounded Sequential Generator and Its Regimes}
   294→\label{sec:intro}
   295→%======================================================================
   296→
   297→This manuscript develops a comparative theory of finite-state approximation
   298→for sequential objects.  The resource is memory, measured by the number of
   299→internal states or, equivalently, by the index of a finite right congruence on
   300→histories.  The cost is a regime-specific distortion functional: Boolean
   301→mismatch for commitment, KL divergence for retention, operator-norm distance
   302→for linear grounding relaxations, and related quantitative costs.
   303→
   304→The framework may be viewed as a rate--distortion theory of bounded sequential
   305→transduction.  The rate is the finite-state budget $M$.  The distortion is the
   306→task loss induced by the regime.
   307→
   308→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   309→\subsection{Bounded sequential generators}
   310→\label{subsec:bsg-intro}
   311→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   312→
   313→A \textbf{Bounded Sequential Generator} (BSG) is a deterministic finite-state
   314→transducer, i.e.\ a Mealy machine,
   315→\[
   316→\mathcal A=(\mathcal S,s_0,\delta,\lambda),
   317→\qquad
   318→|\mathcal S|\le M,
   319→\]
   320→with finite input alphabet $\mathcal I$, finite output alphabet $\mathcal O$,
   321→transition map
   322→\[
   323→\delta:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal S,
   324→\]
   325→and output map
   326→\[
   327→\lambda:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal O.
   328→\]
   329→It defines a synchronous causal function
   330→\[
   331→F_{\mathcal A}:\mathcal I^{\mathbb N}\to\mathcal O^{\mathbb N}.
   332→\]
   333→
   334→The deterministic Mealy BSG is the machine model for the commitment regime.
   335→In that regime, the target is a deterministic specification, a safety
   336→property, or a deterministic strategic rule, and the question is whether a
   337→finite-state machine implements or enforces it exactly or approximately.  The
   338→exact zero-error theory is governed by Myhill--Nerode residual equivalence.
   339→
   340→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   341→\subsection{Three approximation regimes}
   342→\label{subsec:three-regimes}
   343→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   344→
   345→The manuscript studies three regimes.  They share a finite-state syntax but
   346→differ in their semantic objects.  The framework does not reduce the three
   347→regimes to a single machine category.
   348→
   349→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2.2em]
   350→\item \textbf{Commitment.}
   351→The target is a deterministic residual function.  A history $u$ determines a
   352→residual continuation map
   353→\[
   354→F_u(w)=F(uw)_{|u|+1:|u|+|w|}.
   355→\]
   356→Two histories are equivalent if they induce the same residual function.  The
   357→minimal exact state complexity is the Myhill--Nerode index
   358→\[
   359→\kappa_{\det}(F)
   360→=
   361→\operatorname{index}(\sim_F).
   362→\]
   363→The exact commitment gap is a threshold phenomenon:
   364→\[
   365→\Com(M)=0
   366→\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
   367→M\ge\kappa_{\det}(F).
   368→\]
   369→
   370→\item \textbf{Retention.}
   371→The target is a stochastic controlled process whose predictive structure is
   372→encoded by causal states.  Each positive-mass causal state $s\in\Splus$
   373→carries a predictive distribution $P_s$ over future outputs.  An $M$-state
   374→approximation merges causal states into at most $M$ lumped machine states
   375→through a stationary lumpable quotient.
   376→
   377→The full-KL retention gap is
   378→\[
   379→\RetKL(M)
   380→=
   381→\min_{\substack{
   382→\phi:\Splus\to\mathcal K\ \text{lumpable}\\
   383→|\mathcal K|\le M
   384→}}
   385→\sum_{k\in\mathcal K}
   386→\sum_{s:\phi(s)=k}
   387→\pi_s
   388→D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_s\|\bar P_k),
   389→\]
   390→where
   391→\[
   392→\bar P_k
   393→=
   394→\frac{
   395→\sum_{s:\phi(s)=k}\pi_s P_s
   396→}{
   397→\sum_{s:\phi(s)=k}\pi_s
   398→}.
   399→\]
   400→In synchronized machines, lumpable quotients of $\Splus$ correspond to
   401→finite-index right congruences on sufficiently long histories.  In the general
   402→unifilar model the causal-state update reads the realized output, the feasible
   403→quotients are the unifilar-lumpable ones, and the input-driven synchronized
   404→subclass recovers right congruences on input histories.
   405→
   406→When the predictive family is restricted to common-covariance Gaussians, one
   407→obtains a quadratic restricted gap
   408→\[
   409→\RetQuad(M).
   410→\]
   411→The full-KL gap and the quadratic restricted gap are distinct objects.
   412→
   413→\item \textbf{Grounding.}
   414→The target is a stochastic channel or residual kernel.  The spectral converse
   415→uses linear finite-rank realizations rather than deterministic Mealy
   416→machines.  The relevant object is a Hankel operator $H_\nu$ built from an
   417→impulse-response function.
   418→
   419→The unrestricted linear finite-rank Hankel relaxation gap is
   420→\[
   421→\Dunres(M)
   422→=
   423→\inf_{\operatorname{rank}B\le M}
   424→\norm{H_\nu-B}_{\mathrm{op}},
   425→\]
   426→and satisfies
   427→\[
   428→\Dunres(M)
   429→=
   430→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu)
   431→\]
   432→by Eckart--Young--Mirsky \cite{eckart1936,mirsky1960}.
   433→
   434→The Hankel-structured finite-rank realization gap is
   435→\[
   436→\DHankstr(M)
   437→=
   438→\inf_{\substack{
   439→\operatorname{rank}B\le M\\
   440→B\ \text{Hankel}
   441→}}
   442→\norm{H_\nu-B}_{\mathrm{op}},
   443→\]
   444→and satisfies
   445→\[
   446→\DHankstr(M)
   447→\ge
   448→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\nu).
   449→\]
   450→Equality in the Hankel-structured problem requires additional structure
   451→(Theorems~\ref{thm:aak-equality} and~\ref{thm:aak-multiletter}).  The
   452→resource $M$ here is a finite-rank budget on a bounded operator, not the
   453→index of a right congruence; Section~\ref{subsec:grounding-instantiation} and
   454→the master scope of Section~\ref{subsec:meta-scope} record this distinction
   455→explicitly, and Definition~\ref{def:symbolic-grounding-gap} below supplies a
   456→quotient-typed \emph{symbolic} grounding gap, over deterministic Mealy
   457→machines, whose resource is again a right-congruence index and which is
   458→therefore the object genuinely comparable to commitment and retention.  A
   459→closed-form finite-budget value for that symbolic gap remains open; certified
   460→bounds are given in
   461→Propositions~\ref{prop:grounding-finite-section},~\ref{prop:symbolic-finite-horizon}
   462→and Theorem~\ref{thm:observable-floor}.
   463→\end{enumerate}
   464→
   465→The three regimes share a \textbf{typed finite-memory budget} rather than a
   466→single literal resource.  For commitment and retention (and for the symbolic
   467→grounding gap of Definition~\ref{def:symbolic-grounding-gap}), the budget $M$
   468→is the index of a finite right congruence on histories.  For the
   469→\emph{unrestricted linear} grounding relaxation, the budget $M$ is instead the
   470→rank of a finite-rank operator approximating a Hankel operator.  These two
   471→notions of budget are related --- both cap the ``resolution'' of the
   472→approximant --- but they are not asserted equivalent: a rank-$M$ operator is
   473→not in general reachable through an $M$-state quotient, and an $M$-state
   474→quotient does not in general act on the joint alphabet the way a rank-$M$
   475→Hankel truncation does.  Section~\ref{subsec:meta-scope} makes this typed
   476→picture precise as four pillars common to all three regimes.
   477→
   478→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   479→\subsection{The shared finite-state schema}
   480→\label{subsec:shared-schema-intro}
   481→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   482→
   483→The shared combinatorial resource is the index of a finite quotient of a
   484→regime-typed history system (Definition~\ref{def:history-system}, developed in
   485→Section~\ref{subsec:history-actions}).
   486→Commitment quotients live on input histories $\mathcal I^*$ with a total right
   487→action; unifilar retention quotients live on feasible joint input--output
   488→histories with a pruned action, in the support-relative sense of
   489→Definition~\ref{def:support-right-cong}; grounding quotients live on the joint
   490→alphabet $\Sigma=\mathcal I\times\mathcal O$.  For the commitment regime, and
   491→for the input-driven retention subclass, the classical form is the following;
   492→Remark~\ref{rem:regime-history-instances} tabulates the three instantiations.
   493→
   494→Let $\mathcal I^*$ denote the set of finite input histories.  A right
   495→congruence $\sim$ on $\mathcal I^*$, in the standard sense of automata theory
   496→\cite{sakarovitch2009}, is an equivalence relation satisfying
   497→\[
   498→u\sim v
   499→\implies
   500→ux\sim vx
   501→\qquad
   502→\forall x\in\mathcal I.
   503→\]
   504→Equivalently, the quotient $\mathcal I^*/\!\sim$ carries a well-defined
   505→deterministic transition
   506→\[
   507→[u]\cdot x=[ux].
   508→\]
   509→The index of $\sim$ is the number of equivalence classes.  This index is the
   510→finite-state budget.
   511→
   512→A target sequential object may be represented as a trajectory
   513→\[
   514→\delta:\mathcal I^*\to\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R),
   515→\]
   516→where $\mathbf R$ is a regime-specific residual category.  An $M$-state
   517→approximation factors $\delta$ through a finite quotient:
   518→\[
   519→\mathcal I^*
   520→\longrightarrow
   521→\mathcal I^*/\!\sim
   522→\longrightarrow
   523→\mathbf R,
   524→\qquad
   525→\operatorname{index}(\sim)\le M.
   526→\]
   527→The associated variational gap is
   528→\[
   529→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
   530→=
   531→\inf_{\substack{\sim,\Psi\\ \operatorname{index}(\sim)\le M}}
   532→\mathcal L(\delta,\Psi\circ Q).
   533→\]
   534→
   535→\begin{remark}[Scope of the Quotient Display]
   536→\label{rem:quotient-display-scope}
   537→The display above is the quotient-typed instance of the schema, and it is the
   538→literal resource for commitment, for retention, and for the symbolic
   539→grounding gap of Definition~\ref{def:symbolic-grounding-gap}.  The
   540→\emph{unrestricted linear} grounding relaxation of
   541→Theorem~\ref{thm:spectral-grounding} instead instantiates the schema with a
   542→finite-rank feasible set,
   543→\[
   544→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
   545→=
   546→\inf_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\le M}
   547→\mathcal L(\delta,B),
   548→\]
   549→a genuinely different budget type.  Section~\ref{subsec:meta-scope} records
   550→both instances as coordinates of one typed variational schema without
   551→asserting that the two budgets coincide.
   552→\end{remark}
   553→
   554→Each regime attaches its own per-state semantic object:
   555→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
   556→\item \textbf{commitment:} a residual function;
   557→\item \textbf{retention:} a predictive distribution or exponential-family
   558→parameter;
   559→\item \textbf{grounding:} an operator, kernel, or linear realization
   560→coordinate.
   561→\end{itemize}
   562→
   563→The shared theorems concern the quotient structure and the index of right
   564→congruences.  The analytical engines---Myhill--Nerode theory, information
   565→bottleneck, Hankel approximation, game-theoretic commitment, online learning,
   566→and operator algebra---are regime-specific.
   567→
   568→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   569→\subsection{Stationary aggregation and type-correct costs}
   570→\label{subsec:stationary-intro}
   571→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   572→
   573→The average-case theory uses stationary aggregation.  For each fixed length
   574→$n$, a stationary process assigns total mass one to the prefixes of length $n$:
   575→\[
   576→\sum_{|u|=n}\mu(u)=1.
   577→\]
   578→Consequently,
   579→\[
   580→\sum_{u\in\mathcal I^*}\mu(u)=\infty,
   581→\]
   582→so the prefix weights do not define a probability measure on the whole free
   583→monoid $\mathcal I^*$.  The theory uses stationary aggregation schemes.
   584→
   585→Two canonical average-case aggregations are admitted:
   586→\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\alph*)]
   587→\item a stationary Ces\`aro average;
   588→\item a discounted prefix measure.
   589→\end{enumerate}
   590→
   591→The worst-case aggregation remains the supremum over all finite histories:
   592→\[
   593→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta)
   594→=
   595→\sup_{u\in\mathcal I^*}
   596→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u)).
   597→\]
   598→
   599→The cost profunctor $\mathcal E$ is regime-specific.  Commitment uses a
   600→Boolean or $0/1$ equality cost.  Retention uses KL divergence or a quadratic
   601→Fisher surrogate.  Grounding uses operator-norm distance or a related linear
   602→relaxation cost.  The structural Boolean cost $\{0,\infty\}$ is distinguished
   603→from the normalized operational cost $\{0,1\}$.
   604→
   605→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   606→\subsection{Analytical boundaries}
   607→\label{subsec:boundaries-intro}
   608→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   609→
   610→The framework separates shared syntax from regime-specific analysis.
   611→
   612→\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.0em]
   613→\item \textbf{Shared syntax versus regime-specific semantics.}
   614→The schema provides histories, right congruences, finite quotients, and
   615→variational gaps.  It does not derive the Myhill--Nerode theorem, the
   616→Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem, or the information-bottleneck identity from
   617→categorical axioms alone.
   618→
   619→\item \textbf{Exact theorems versus local theorems.}
   620→The commitment zero-error threshold, the full-KL finite-state
   621→information-bottleneck identity, and the unrestricted linear Hankel spectral
   622→converse are exact within their domains.  The full-KL spectral lower bound is
   623→local and requires explicit regularity assumptions.  The Hankel-structured
   624→finite-rank realization gap satisfies the unrestricted singular-value lower
   625→bound, but equality requires additional structure.
   626→
   627→\item \textbf{Converses versus achievability.}
   628→Spectral converses are lower bounds on approximation gaps.  They become
   629→online regret upper rates only when paired with explicit approximation
   630→achievability bounds.
   631→
   632→\item \textbf{Linear relaxations versus discrete problems.}
   633→The linear Hankel gap and the linear Price-of-Safety surrogate are exact
   634→linear objects.  They do not automatically solve the corresponding discrete
   635→right-congruence optimization problems.
   636→
   637→\item \textbf{Heuristics versus theorems.}
   638→The spectral-tail picture is a heuristic.  The tail shape depends on
   639→aggregation, operator type, affine versus linear approximation, rank versus
   640→state count, compactness, domination, and quadratic-regime assumptions.
   641→
   642→\item \textbf{No unconditional cross-regime ordering.}
   643→The framework does not assert a universal inequality among grounding,
   644→retention, and commitment gaps.  The Independence Theorem shows that the
   645→gaps can be varied independently.
   646→\end{itemize}
   647→

   649→\subsection{Components of the framework}
   650→\label{subsec:integrated-components-intro}
   651→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   652→
   653→The framework comprises the following components.
   654→
   655→\paragraph{Schatten conditional converse template.}
   656→A task theory with a rank-controlled response operator $A_\delta$ and a
   657→Schatten-$p$ domination inequality satisfies a unified converse:
   658→\[
   659→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
   660→\ge
   661→\frac1{c_p}
   662→\Phi^{(p)}_{r_{\mathbb T}(M)}(A_\delta),
   663→\]
   664→where $r_{\mathbb T}(M)$ is the effective rank budget and
   665→$\Phi^{(p)}_r$ is the Schatten-$p$ tail.
   666→
   667→Grounding is the Schatten-$\infty$ instance for the unrestricted linear
   668→finite-rank relaxation; retention is the Schatten-$1$ instance with effective
   669→rank $M-1$; commitment is the Boolean-rank vertex.  Contextwise $\ell^p$
   670→aggregation does not by itself imply Schatten-$p$ domination.
   671→
   672→\paragraph{Adaptive two-axis oracle inequality.}
   673→Within each type-correct regime, offline approximation and online estimation
   674→compose into a bias--variance decomposition:
   675→\[
   676→\Reg_T(\mathcal A_M)
   677→\le
   678→\Aapp^{\mathsf r}(M)
   679→+
   680→\Est_M^{\mathsf r}(T).
   681→\]
   682→Adaptive aggregation over the state budget $M$ yields an oracle inequality
   683→without knowledge of the optimal budget.  For bounded agnostic losses, the
   684→adaptive penalty is
   685→\[
   686→\PsiAgg_M(T)
   687→=
   688→O\!\left(
   689→\sqrt{T\ln(eM)}
   690→+
   691→\ln(eM)
   692→\right).
   693→\]
   694→Realizable $0/1$ and mixable log-loss model selection have logarithmic
   695→overhead.  Spectral tails enter online rates only under explicit spectral-rate
   696→assumptions.
   697→
   698→Estimation rates may be supplied by a Littlestone bound, a multiclass
   699→Littlestone bound, a mixability bound, or a linear-regret bound.  The oracle
   700→inequality does not assume a single estimation rate across regimes.
   701→
   702→\paragraph{Linear Price-of-Safety surrogate.}
   703→For a quadratic/Fisher retention embedding with state-indexed centered Gram
   704→matrix
   705→\[
   706→G_{st}
   707→=
   708→\sqrt{\pi_s\pi_t}\,
   709→\langle y_s-\bar y,\;y_t-\bar y\rangle,
   710→\]
   711→and a safety partition inducing a pinching conditional expectation
   712→$\EA$, the linear block-local Price-of-Safety surrogate is
   713→\[
   714→\PoSlin(M)
   715→=
   716→\frac12\KyF{G}{M-1}
   717→-
   718→\frac12\KyF{\EA G}{M-1}.
   719→\]
   720→Pinching majorization gives nonnegativity:
   721→\[
   722→\PoSlin(M)\ge0.
   723→\]
   724→The coupling bound
   725→\[
   726→\PoSlin(M)
   727→\le
   728→\frac12\KyF{G-\EA G}{M-1}
   729→\]
   730→is an upper bound, not an equality.  This surrogate is exact for the linear
   731→block-local problem but is not the discrete right-congruence Price of Safety
   732→without relaxation-error control.
   733→
   734→\paragraph{Exponent vertex correspondence.}
   735→The manuscript separates three exponent-like layers:
   736→\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\arabic*)]
   737→\item R\'enyi order $\alpha$;
   738→\item Schatten exponent $p$;
   739→\item aggregation or vertex label.
   740→\end{enumerate}
   741→
   742→Retention is the $\alpha=1$ KL vertex.  Commitment is recovered from the
   743→$\alpha=0$ support vertex after symmetrization and Boolean valuation.
   744→Grounding is organized by the $\alpha=\infty$ supremum vertex, but the exact
   745→unrestricted linear finite-rank grounding cost is operator-norm distance, not
   746→max-divergence.
   747→
   748→The equality of vertex labels
   749→\[
   750→\alpha=p\in\{0,1,\infty\}
   751→\]
   752→is a formal consistency, not a derivation of the Mirsky converses from
   753→R\'enyi identities.

   783→\section{The Schema of Finite-State Approximation}
   784→\label{sec:schema}
   785→%======================================================================
   786→
   787→This section develops the shared finite-state schema.  The schema supplies a
   788→common syntax for all regimes: histories, enriched cost categories, finite
   789→right congruences, quotient machines, stationary aggregation, and profinite
   790→completion.  The analytical meaning of the residual objects is supplied later
   791→by each regime.

   793→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   794→\subsection{The Enriching Poset}
   795→\label{subsec:enriching-poset}
   796→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   797→
   798→All costs take values in the monoidal poset
   799→\[
   800→\mathbf V=([0,\infty],\ge,+,0).
   801→\]
   802→The unit is $0$, and the order is reversed from the usual real order: a
   803→morphism $v\to v'$ exists iff $v\ge v'$ in the usual order.  Thus $0$ is
   804→terminal and $\infty$ is initial.  A morphism $v\to v'$ records that the cost
   805→has decreased from $v$ to $v'$.
   806→
   807→The Boolean sub-poset is
   808→\[
   809→\mathbf V_{\mathbb B}=\{0,\infty\}\subset[0,\infty].
   810→\]
   811→It corresponds to exact equality versus definite mismatch.  A normalized
   812→Boolean mismatch cost with values $\{0,1\}$ is treated separately as an
   813→operational corollary.  The structural zero-error theory uses
   814→$\{0,\infty\}$; the normalized decision-theoretic version uses $\{0,1\}$.
   815→
   816→The reversed-order convention is the standard Lawvere metric convention
   817→\cite{lawvere1973}.
   818→
   819→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   820→\subsection{$\mathbf V$-Categories and $\mathbf V$-Profunctors}
   821→\label{subsec:V-cats-profunctors}
   822→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   823→
   824→A \textbf{$\mathbf V$-category} $\mathbf R$ consists of a set of objects
   825→$\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)$ and hom-objects
   826→\[
   827→\mathbf R(X,Y)\in\mathbf V
   828→\]
   829→satisfying:
   830→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
   831→\item \textbf{Identity.}
   832→\[
   833→0\ge \mathbf R(X,X),
   834→\]
   835→hence $\mathbf R(X,X)=0$.
   836→
   837→\item \textbf{Composition.}
   838→\[
   839→\mathbf R(X,Y)+\mathbf R(Y,Z)\ge \mathbf R(X,Z).
   840→\]
   841→\end{enumerate}
   842→
   843→A \textbf{$\mathbf V$-profunctor}
   844→\[
   845→\mathcal E:\mathbf R^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathbf R\to\mathbf V
   846→\]
   847→assigns costs $\mathcal E(X,Y)$ with left and right actions satisfying
   848→\[
   849→\mathbf R(X',X)+\mathcal E(X,Y)\ge \mathcal E(X',Y),
   850→\]
   851→and
   852→\[
   853→\mathcal E(X,Y)+\mathbf R(Y,Y')\ge \mathcal E(X,Y').
   854→\]
   855→
   856→The profunctor $\mathcal E$ is the cost bimodule.  It measures the mismatch
   857→between residual objects $X$ and $Y$, while the left and right actions express
   858→compatibility with the internal geometry of $\mathbf R$.
   859→
   860→\begin{remark}[Discrete versus enriched residuals]
   861→\label{rem:discrete-vs-enriched}
   862→For retention and commitment, $\mathbf R$ is usually a discrete
   863→$\mathbf V$-category:
   864→\[
   865→\mathbf R(X,Y)=
   866→\begin{cases}
   867→0,&X=Y,\\
   868→\infty,&X\neq Y.
   869→\end{cases}
   870→\]
   871→Then the action inequalities impose no triangle inequality on $\mathcal E$.
   872→KL divergence is not a metric, and Boolean mismatch is not a metric.
   873→
   874→For grounding, $\mathbf R$ may be a metric $\mathbf V$-category of operators,
   875→with
   876→\[
   877→\mathbf R(X,Y)=
   878→\norm{X-Y}_{\mathrm{op}}.
   879→\]
   880→If $\mathcal E$ is the identity profunctor,
   881→\[
   882→\mathcal E(X,Y)=\mathbf R(X,Y),
   883→\]
   884→then the action inequalities reduce to the triangle inequality for the
   885→operator norm.
   886→\end{remark}
   887→
   888→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   889→\subsection{The History $\mathbf V$-Category}
   890→\label{subsec:history-category}
   891→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   892→
   893→Let $\mathcal I$ be a finite input alphabet.  The \textbf{history
   894→$\mathbf V$-category} $\mathbf H$ is the discrete $\mathbf V$-category on the
   895→free monoid $\mathcal I^*$:
   896→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
   897→\item objects are finite words $u\in\mathcal I^*$;
   898→\item hom-objects are
   899→\[
   900→\mathbf H(u,v)=
   901→\begin{cases}
   902→0,&u=v,\\
   903→\infty,&u\neq v.
   904→\end{cases}
   905→\]
   906→\end{itemize}
   907→
   908→Because $\mathbf H$ is discrete, any object-level map
   909→\[
   910→\delta:\mathcal I^*\to\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)
   911→\]
   912→is automatically a $\mathbf V$-functor.  No nontrivial morphism inequalities
   913→need to be checked.
   914→
   915→The prefix structure of histories is not encoded in the hom-objects of
   916→$\mathbf H$.  It is encoded externally in the right-congruence condition,
   917→which ensures that deterministic finite-state approximations evolve
   918→consistently under input extensions.
   919→
   920→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   921→\subsection{Task Theory with Stationary Aggregation}
   922→\label{subsec:task-theory}
   923→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
   924→
   925→\begin{definition}[Task Theory]
   926→\label{def:task-theory}
   927→A \textbf{task theory} is a tuple
   928→\[
   929→\mathbb T=(\mathbf H,\mathbf R,\mathcal E,\delta,\mathrm{Agg},\mathcal L)
   930→\]
   931→where:
   932→\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\arabic*)]
   933→\item $\mathbf H$ is the history $\mathbf V$-category.
   934→\item $\mathbf R$ is a $\mathbf V$-category of residual objects.
   935→\item $\mathcal E:\mathbf R^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathbf R\to\mathbf V$ is a
   936→cost profunctor which is \textbf{reflexive}: $\mathcal E(X,X)=0$ for every
   937→$X\in\mathbf R$.  Reflexivity is not automatic for a $\mathbf V$-profunctor.
   938→In the Lawvere quantale $([0,\infty],\ge,+)$ the constant profunctor
   939→$\mathcal E\equiv1$ on a one-object $\mathbf R$ satisfies every profunctor
   940→axiom, and for it the trajectory cannot be realized at any budget even though
   941→its Nerode index is $1$.  Every cost used in this manuscript --- KL divergence,
   942→operator norm, and the $0/1$ commitment loss --- is reflexive, so the axiom
   943→constrains nothing in the instances but is required for the generic statements
   944→of Meta-Theorem~\ref{meta:monotone}.
   945→\item $\delta:\mathbf H\to\mathbf R$ is the true trajectory.
   946→\item $\mathrm{Agg}$ is an aggregation scheme, one of:
   947→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
   948→\item a stationary Ces\`aro average;
   949→\item a discounted prefix measure; or
   950→\item the worst-case supremum
   951→$\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta)=\sup_{u\in\mathcal I^*}
   952→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u))$.
   953→\end{itemize}
   954→The first two are \emph{average-case} schemes and require the stationary or
   955→discounted datum; the third requires none.  A task theory carrying the
   956→worst-case scheme is called \emph{uniform}.  Results are stated against a
   957→declared scheme, since the Boolean dichotomy in particular takes different
   958→forms under worst-case and average-case aggregation
   959→(Meta-Theorem~\ref{meta:boolean}).
   960→\item $\mathcal L$ is the resulting cost functional.
   961→\end{enumerate}
   962→\end{definition}
   963→
   964→\begin{definition}[Stationary Ces\`aro aggregation]
   965→\label{def:cesaro-agg}
   966→Let $U_t$ denote the stationary past at time $t$.  For a cost process
   967→\[
   968→c_t=\mathcal E(\delta(U_t),\widehat\delta(U_t)),
   969→\]
   970→define
   971→\[
   972→\overline{\mathcal L}_1(\delta,\widehat\delta)
   973→=
   974→\lim_{T\to\infty}
   975→\frac1T
   976→\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
   977→\mathbb E[c_t],
   978→\]
   979→whenever the limit exists.
   980→\end{definition}
   981→
   982→For finite-state stationary ergodic processes and finite-state
   983→approximations, the limit exists under standard ergodicity assumptions.  In
   984→non-ergodic settings, one may replace the limit by a limsup or by ergodic
   985→decomposition.
   986→
   987→\begin{definition}[Discounted prefix aggregation]
   988→\label{def:discounted-agg}
   989→For $0<\gamma<1$, define a probability measure on $\mathcal I^*$ by
   990→\[
   991→\mu_\gamma(u)
   992→=
   993→(1-\gamma)\gamma^{|u|}
   994→\mathbb P(X_{1:|u|}=u).
   995→\]
   996→Then
   997→\[
   998→\mathcal L_1^\gamma(\delta,\widehat\delta)
   999→=
  1000→\mathbb E_{u\sim\mu_\gamma}
  1001→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u)).
  1002→\]
  1003→\end{definition}
  1004→
  1005→The discounted prefix measure is a probability measure because
  1006→\[
  1007→\sum_{u\in\mathcal I^*}\mu_\gamma(u)
  1008→=
  1009→(1-\gamma)
  1010→\sum_{n=0}^\infty
  1011→\gamma^n
  1012→\sum_{|u|=n}
  1013→\mathbb P(X_{1:n}=u)
  1014→=
  1015→(1-\gamma)
  1016→\sum_{n=0}^\infty
  1017→\gamma^n
  1018→=
  1019→1.
  1020→\]
  1021→
  1022→The worst-case aggregation remains
  1023→\[
  1024→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta)
  1025→=
  1026→\sup_{u\in\mathcal I^*}
  1027→\mathcal E(\delta(u),\widehat\delta(u)).
  1028→\]
  1029→It is not an average-case aggregation and should not be conflated with the
  1030→stationary Ces\`aro or discounted prefix schemes.
  1031→
  1032→\begin{remark}[Finite Prefixes versus Stationary States]
  1033→\label{rem:prefixes-versus-states}
  1034→Finite prefixes do not define a probability measure on all of
  1035→$\mathcal I^*$.  For stationary retention, the canonical object is the
  1036→stationary state process $(S_t)$ and its stationary support $\Splus$.  Finite
  1037→histories enter directly in synchronized machines, where $S_t$ is a function
  1038→of a finite suffix of the input past.
  1039→\end{remark}
  1040→
  1041→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1042→\subsection{Regime-Typed History Actions}
  1043→\label{subsec:history-actions}
  1044→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1045→
  1046→The finite-state resource shared by the three regimes is the index of a finite
  1047→quotient of a history system.  Which history system is regime-dependent: the
  1048→regimes share the \emph{form} of a finite-index right-action quotient, not a
  1049→single literal free monoid.  Forcing every regime onto $\mathcal I^*$ would
  1050→misdescribe predictive machines whose state update reads the realized output.
  1051→
  1052→\begin{definition}[History System]
  1053→\label{def:history-system}
  1054→A \textbf{history system} is a tuple
  1055→\[
  1056→\mathsf H=(H,h_\varnothing,\mathcal A,\cdot),
  1057→\]
  1058→where $H$ is a set of finite histories with root $h_\varnothing$, $\mathcal A$
  1059→is an event alphabet, and $\cdot$ is a partial right action
  1060→\[
  1061→H\times\mathcal A\rightharpoonup H,
  1062→\qquad
  1063→(h,a)\mapsto h\cdot a,
  1064→\]
  1065→defined exactly when $a$ is \emph{feasible} from $h$; write
  1066→$\mathrm{Feas}(h)\subseteq\mathcal A$ for the set of feasible events.  The
  1067→action is \emph{pruned}: whenever $h\cdot a$ is defined it lies in $H$.  If
  1068→$\mathrm{Feas}(h)=\mathcal A$ for every $h$, the action is \emph{total}.
  1069→\end{definition}
  1070→
  1071→\begin{definition}[Support-Relative Right Congruence]
  1072→\label{def:support-right-cong}
  1073→Let $\mathsf H=(H,h_\varnothing,\mathcal A,\cdot)$ be a history system.  A
  1074→\textbf{support-relative right congruence} on $\mathsf H$ is an equivalence
  1075→relation $\sim$ on $H$ such that $u\sim v$ implies
  1076→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
  1077→\item $\mathrm{Feas}(u)=\mathrm{Feas}(v)$; and
  1078→\item $u\cdot a\sim v\cdot a$ for every $a\in\mathrm{Feas}(u)$.
  1079→\end{enumerate}
  1080→Its \textbf{index} is the number of equivalence classes.  The quotient
  1081→$H/\!\sim$ carries a well-defined partial transition $[u]\cdot a=[u\cdot a]$,
  1082→with $\mathrm{Feas}([u])=\mathrm{Feas}(u)$; clause~(i) is exactly what makes
  1083→the feasible-event set of a class well defined.  For a total action clause~(i)
  1084→is vacuous and the definition reduces to Definition~\ref{def:right-cong}.
  1085→\end{definition}
  1086→
  1087→\begin{remark}[Clause~(i) Is Not Redundant]
  1088→\label{rem:support-clause-i-needed}
  1089→Clause~(ii) alone, read as a condition on commonly feasible events, does not
  1090→give a quotient history system.  Take $\mathcal A=\{a,b\}$ and two histories
  1091→$u,v$ with $\mathrm{Feas}(u)=\{a\}$ and $\mathrm{Feas}(v)=\{b\}$.  The
  1092→relation identifying $u$ with $v$ satisfies clause~(ii) vacuously, there being
  1093→no commonly feasible event, yet the class $[u]$ has no well-defined feasible
  1094→set and the quotient carries no transition structure.  This is the mechanism
  1095→behind Remark~\ref{rem:unifilar-support-not-automatic} below.
  1096→\end{remark}
  1097→
  1098→\begin{remark}[The Regimes as History Systems]
  1099→\label{rem:regime-history-instances}
  1100→The principal instantiations are the following.
  1101→
  1102→\begin{center}
  1103→\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{3.1cm}l@{}}
  1104→\toprule
  1105→regime & $H$ & $\mathcal A$ & action \\
  1106→\midrule
  1107→commitment & $\mathcal I^*$ & $\mathcal I$ & total \\
  1108→retention, unifilar & feasible joint histories
  1109→& $\mathcal I\times\mathcal O$ & pruned \\
  1110→retention, input-driven & $\mathcal I^*$ & $\mathcal I$ & total \\
  1111→grounding & $\Sigma^*$, $\Sigma=\mathcal I\times\mathcal O$ & $\Sigma$ & total \\
  1112→\bottomrule
  1113→\end{tabular}
  1114→\end{center}
  1115→
  1116→For commitment and grounding the action is total and
  1117→Definition~\ref{def:right-cong} applies verbatim.  For unifilar retention the
  1118→feasible joint histories form a pruned tree under the stationary law, and
  1119→compatibility with the action is understood in the support-relative sense.  The
  1120→input-driven model of Definition~\ref{def:controlled-markov} is the case in
  1121→which the state process factors through the input projection, and the
  1122→input-history syntax is recovered.  The history $\mathbf V$-category of
  1123→Section~\ref{subsec:history-category} is built on whichever $H$ the regime
  1124→supplies; being discrete, it makes every object-level trajectory a
  1125→$\mathbf V$-functor regardless of which system is used.
  1126→\end{remark}
  1127→
  1128→\begin{lemma}[Cofilteredness for Support-Relative Congruences]
  1129→\label{lem:cofiltered-support}
  1130→Let $\sim_1,\sim_2$ be finite-index support-relative right congruences on a
  1131→history system $\mathsf H$.  Then $\sim_1\cap\sim_2$ is one as well, refines
  1132→both, and has index at most
  1133→$\operatorname{index}(\sim_1)\operatorname{index}(\sim_2)$.  The finite
  1134→quotients of $\mathsf H$ therefore form a cofiltered system.
  1135→\end{lemma}
  1136→
  1137→\begin{proof}
  1138→The intersection of equivalence relations is an equivalence relation.  If
  1139→$u\,(\sim_1\cap\sim_2)\,v$ then $\mathrm{Feas}(u)=\mathrm{Feas}(v)$ already
  1140→from $\sim_1$, giving clause~(i); and for $a$ in that common feasible set,
  1141→$u\cdot a\sim_iv\cdot a$ for $i=1,2$, giving clause~(ii).  The map
  1142→$[u]_{\sim_1\cap\sim_2}\mapsto([u]_{\sim_1},[u]_{\sim_2})$ is well defined and
  1143→injective, which is the index bound.  The argument is that of
  1144→Lemma~\ref{lem:cofiltered}, with the feasible-set clause carried along.
  1145→\end{proof}
  1146→
  1147→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1148→\subsection{Right Congruences and Quotients}
  1149→\label{sec:right-cong}
  1150→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1151→
  1152→\begin{definition}[Right Congruence]
  1153→\label{def:right-cong}
  1154→A \textbf{right congruence} $\sim$ on $\mathbf H$ is an equivalence relation
  1155→on $\mathcal I^*$ such that
  1156→\[
  1157→u\sim v
  1158→\implies
  1159→uw\sim vw
  1160→\quad
  1161→\forall w\in\mathcal I^*.
  1162→\]
  1163→Equivalently, it suffices to require
  1164→\[
  1165→u\sim v
  1166→\implies
  1167→ux\sim vx
  1168→\quad
  1169→\forall x\in\mathcal I.
  1170→\]
  1171→The \textbf{index} of $\sim$ is the number of equivalence classes.
  1172→\end{definition}
  1173→
  1174→This is the total-action instance of Definition~\ref{def:support-right-cong};
  1175→pruned retention history systems use the support-relative form.
  1176→
  1177→The quotient $\mathbf Q=\mathbf H/\!\sim$ has objects $[u]$ and hom-objects
  1178→\[
  1179→\mathbf Q([u],[v])=
  1180→\begin{cases}
  1181→0,&[u]=[v],\\
  1182→\infty,&\text{otherwise}.
  1183→\end{cases}
  1184→\]
  1185→The quotient map
  1186→\[
  1187→Q:\mathbf H\to\mathbf Q,
  1188→\qquad
  1189→u\mapsto[u],
  1190→\]
  1191→is a $\mathbf V$-functor.  The right-congruence condition ensures that the
  1192→quotient carries a well-defined deterministic transition
  1193→\[
  1194→[u]\cdot x=[ux].
  1195→\]
  1196→
  1197→\begin{definition}[$M$-State Approximation]
  1198→\label{def:M-state-approx}
  1199→An \textbf{$M$-state approximation} of a task theory $\mathbb T$ is a pair
  1200→$(\sim,\Psi)$ where $\sim$ is a right congruence of index at most $M$ and
  1201→\[
  1202→\Psi:\mathbf Q\to\mathbf R
  1203→\]
  1204→is a $\mathbf V$-functor.  The approximate trajectory is
  1205→\[
  1206→\Psi\circ Q:\mathbf H\to\mathbf R.
  1207→\]
  1208→\end{definition}
  1209→
  1210→\begin{definition}[$M$-State Gap]
  1211→\label{def:M-state-gap}
  1212→The \textbf{$M$-state gap} is
  1213→\[
  1214→\Delta_{\mathbb T}(M)
  1215→=
  1216→\inf_{\substack{\sim,\Psi\\ \operatorname{index}(\sim)\le M}}
  1217→\mathcal L(\delta,\Psi\circ Q).
  1218→\]
  1219→\end{definition}
  1220→
  1221→\begin{remark}[Machine type depends on $\mathbf R$]
  1222→\label{rem:realization-types}
  1223→The composite $\Psi\circ Q$ has deterministic state-transition structure, but
  1224→its per-state content depends on $\mathbf R$:
  1225→\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  1226→\item \textbf{Commitment:} each state stores a residual function; the
  1227→approximant is a deterministic Mealy BSG.
  1228→\item \textbf{Retention:} each state stores a predictive distribution or
  1229→kernel; the approximant is a unifilar lumped controlled predictor
  1230→(Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-lumpable}), and in the input-driven synchronized
  1231→subclass a deterministic-transition, stochastic-emission machine, in the sense
  1232→of Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-machine} and
  1233→Remark~\ref{rem:unifilar-proper-subclass}.
  1234→\item \textbf{Grounding spectral converse:} each state stores an operator or
  1235→linear coordinate; the approximant is a linear finite-rank realization.
  1236→\end{itemize}
  1237→The shared theorems concern $\operatorname{index}(\sim)$.  The
  1238→regime-specific theorems concern the semantic objects attached to the states.
  1239→\end{remark}
  1240→
  1241→\begin{remark}[Stationary Retention Quotients]
  1242→\label{rem:schema-stationary-retention}
  1243→For stationary controlled unifilar machines, the primary feasible objects are
  1244→unifilar-lumpable quotients
  1245→\[
  1246→\phi:\Splus\to\mathcal K
  1247→\]
  1248→(Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-lumpable}).  In a machine with joint-history
  1249→synchronization depth $L$, each such quotient with block-uniform support
  1250→induces a support-relative right congruence on feasible joint histories of
  1251→length at least $L$ by
  1252→\[
  1253→u\sim_\phi v
  1254→\iff
  1255→\phi(\sigma(u))=\phi(\sigma(v))
  1256→\]
  1257→(Proposition~\ref{prop:unifilar-lumpability}); in the input-driven
  1258→synchronized subclass this projects to the input-history right congruence of
  1259→Proposition~\ref{prop:lumpability}.  Thus the finite-state budget remains an
  1260→index resource, now on regime-typed histories.  For non-synchronized
  1261→stationary machines, finite-index right congruences on finite histories should
  1262→be replaced by finite-state factors of the stationary state process.
  1263→\end{remark}
  1264→
  1265→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1266→\subsection{Profinite Completion}
  1267→\label{subsec:profinite-completion}
  1268→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1269→
  1270→The finite quotients of $\mathbf H$ form a cofiltered system; the
  1271→support-relative analogue, for pruned history systems, is
  1272→Lemma~\ref{lem:cofiltered-support}.  Let $\mathcal C$ be the cofiltered poset
  1273→of finite-index right congruences on $\mathcal I^*$, ordered by refinement:
  1274→\[
  1275→\sim_1\le\sim_2
  1276→\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  1277→\sim_1\text{ refines }\sim_2.
  1278→\]
  1279→
  1280→\begin{lemma}[$\mathcal C$ Is Cofiltered]
  1281→\label{lem:cofiltered}
  1282→If $\sim_1$ and $\sim_2$ are finite-index right congruences on $\mathcal I^*$,
  1283→then so is $\sim_1\cap\sim_2$, and it refines both.
  1284→\end{lemma}
  1285→
  1286→\begin{proof}
  1287→The intersection is an equivalence relation.  It is a right congruence: if
  1288→$u\sim_1v$ and $u\sim_2v$, then for every $w\in\mathcal I^*$ we have
  1289→$uw\sim_1vw$ and $uw\sim_2vw$, hence
  1290→$uw\,(\sim_1\cap\sim_2)\,vw$.  The map
  1291→\[
  1292→[u]_{\sim_1\cap\sim_2}
  1293→\mapsto
  1294→\bigl([u]_{\sim_1},\,[u]_{\sim_2}\bigr)
  1295→\]
  1296→is well defined and injective, so
  1297→\[
  1298→\operatorname{index}(\sim_1\cap\sim_2)
  1299→\le
  1300→\operatorname{index}(\sim_1)\,
  1301→\operatorname{index}(\sim_2)
  1302→<
  1303→\infty.
  1304→\]
  1305→Thus $\sim_1\cap\sim_2\in\mathcal C$ refines both, so $\mathcal C$ is
  1306→cofiltered.
  1307→\end{proof}
  1308→
  1309→\begin{definition}[Profinite completion]
  1310→\label{def:profinite-completion}
  1311→The \textbf{profinite completion} of $\mathbf H$ is
  1312→\[
  1313→\widehat{\mathbf H}
  1314→=
  1315→\varprojlim_{\sim\in\mathcal C}
  1316→\mathbf H/\!\sim.
  1317→\]
  1318→\end{definition}
  1319→
  1320→An object of $\widehat{\mathbf H}$ is a compatible family
  1321→\[
  1322→(q_\sim)_\sim,
  1323→\qquad
  1324→q_\sim\in\mathbf H/\!\sim,
  1325→\]
  1326→such that whenever $\sim_1$ refines $\sim_2$, the projection of
  1327→$q_{\sim_1}$ to $\mathbf H/\!\sim_2$ equals $q_{\sim_2}$.
  1328→
  1329→There is a canonical dense embedding
  1330→\[
  1331→\iota:\mathbf H\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathbf H},
  1332→\qquad
  1333→u\mapsto([u]_\sim)_\sim.
  1334→\]
  1335→Injectivity is not formal: it holds because the free monoid $\mathcal I^*$ is
  1336→\emph{residually finite} with respect to finite-index right congruences.
  1337→Given distinct $u\ne v$, set $N>\max\{|u|,|v|\}$ and let $\sim_N$ identify two
  1338→words when they are equal, or when both have length at least $N$.  This is a
  1339→right congruence: if $w\sim_Nw'$ and $x\in\mathcal I$ then either $w=w'$, whence
  1340→$wx=w'x$, or both $|w|,|w'|\ge N$, whence both $|wx|,|w'x|\ge N$.  It has
  1341→finite index $|\mathcal I^{<N}|+1$, and separates $u$ from $v$.  Hence the
  1342→components of $\iota(u)$ and $\iota(v)$ differ at $\sim_N$.
  1343→
  1344→The same separation argument applies to pruned history systems, once
  1345→feasibility is carried along.
  1346→
  1347→\begin{proposition}[Residual Finiteness of Synchronized Unifilar History Trees]
  1348→\label{prop:unifilar-residually-finite}
  1349→Let a unifilar machine (Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-machine}) have
  1350→joint-history synchronization, so that its feasible histories of length at
  1351→least $L$ form a pruned history system $F$ carrying a state readout
  1352→$\sigma:F\to\Splus$ with $\sigma(u\cdot a)=\tau(\sigma(u),a)$ on feasible
  1353→events.  Then $F$ is residually finite with respect to finite-index
  1354→support-relative right congruences: the canonical map from $F$ into the inverse
  1355→limit of its finite quotients is injective.
  1356→\end{proposition}
  1357→
  1358→\begin{proof}
  1359→Let $u\neq v$ in $F$.  Choose $N>\max\{|u|,|v|\}$ and define $\sim_N$ by
  1360→declaring $w\sim_Nw'$ when either $w=w'$, or $|w|,|w'|\ge N$ and
  1361→$\sigma(w)=\sigma(w')$.
  1362→
  1363→\emph{Finite index.}  There are at most
  1364→$\sum_{n<N}|\mathcal I\times\mathcal O|^{\,n}$ singleton classes of length
  1365→below $N$ and at most $|\Splus|$ state-labelled classes at length $N$ or
  1366→above.
  1367→
  1368→\emph{Clause~(i).}  Feasibility of $(x,y)$ from a history $w$ means
  1369→$P_{\sigma(w)}^x(\{y\})>0$, so it is a function of $\sigma(w)$ alone.  Two
  1370→histories identified by $\sim_N$ are either equal or have equal $\sigma$, and
  1371→in both cases have the same feasible events.
  1372→
  1373→\emph{Clause~(ii).}  In the identity case $w\cdot a=w'\cdot a$.  Otherwise
  1374→$|w|,|w'|\ge N$ and $\sigma(w)=\sigma(w')$, so for a feasible $(x,y)$,
  1375→\[
  1376→\sigma\bigl(w\cdot(x,y)\bigr)
  1377→=
  1378→\tau(\sigma(w),x,y)
  1379→=
  1380→\tau(\sigma(w'),x,y)
  1381→=
  1382→\sigma\bigl(w'\cdot(x,y)\bigr),
  1383→\]
  1384→and both extensions have length at least $N+1$, hence
  1385→$w\cdot(x,y)\sim_Nw'\cdot(x,y)$.
  1386→
  1387→Since $N$ exceeds $|u|$ and $|v|$, both are singleton classes of $\sim_N$ and
  1388→are therefore separated, so the corresponding components of their images in
  1389→the inverse limit differ.
  1390→\end{proof}
  1391→
  1392→For total-action regimes the completion is that of the free monoid; for
  1393→synchronized unifilar retention it is the completion of the feasible
  1394→joint-history tree, residually finite by
  1395→Proposition~\ref{prop:unifilar-residually-finite}.
  1396→
  1397→The \textbf{profinite uniformity} on $\mathbf H$ is the initial uniformity
  1398→with respect to the projections
  1399→\[
  1400→\mathbf H\to\mathbf H/\!\sim.
  1401→\]
  1402→Equivalently, its basic entourages are
  1403→\[
  1404→U_\sim
  1405→=
  1406→\{(u,v)\in\mathcal I^*\times\mathcal I^*:u\sim v\},
  1407→\]
  1408→where $\sim$ ranges over finite-index right congruences.
  1409→
  1410→\begin{remark}[Cofiltered limits and Lawvere's framework]
  1411→\label{rem:lawvere-limit}
  1412→The enriched poset
  1413→\[
  1414→\mathbf V=([0,\infty],\ge,+,0)
  1415→\]
  1416→is complete symmetric monoidal closed in the sense of Lawvere
  1417→\cite{lawvere1973}.  The relevant enriched cofiltered limits therefore exist.
  1418→Because $\mathbf V$ is posetal, the enriched inverse limit coincides with the
  1419→ordinary compatible-family limit on objects, equipped with the induced
  1420→profinite uniformity.
  1421→\end{remark}
  1422→
  1423→\begin{remark}[Profinite uniformity for discrete residual categories]
  1424→\label{rem:profinite-discrete}
  1425→Let $\mathbf R$ be a discrete $\mathbf V$-category.  Uniform continuity of a
  1426→trajectory
  1427→\[
  1428→\delta:\mathbf H\to\mathbf R
  1429→\]
  1430→in the profinite uniformity is equivalent to $\delta$ factoring through some
  1431→finite quotient:
  1432→\[
  1433→\mathbf H
  1434→\longrightarrow
  1435→\mathbf H/\!\sim
  1436→\longrightarrow
  1437→\mathbf R
  1438→\]
  1439→for some finite-index right congruence $\sim$.  Indeed, in a discrete
  1440→$\mathbf V$-category the only entourages are those containing the diagonal, so
  1441→uniform continuity requires a finite-index congruence $\sim$ with
  1442→$u\sim v\Rightarrow\delta(u)=\delta(v)$, which is exactly factorization through
  1443→$\mathbf H/\!\sim$.
  1444→\end{remark}
  1445→
  1446→\begin{remark}[Compactness and convergence]
  1447→\label{rem:profinite-compact}
  1448→Each finite quotient $\mathbf H/\!\sim$ is a finite discrete space.  The
  1449→inverse limit $\widehat{\mathbf H}$ is compact in the profinite topology.
  1450→This compactness underlies the profinite convergence meta-theorem: under
  1451→compact-uniform hypotheses on $\mathbf R$ and $\mathcal E$, uniform continuity
  1452→of $\delta$ is equivalent to continuous extension to $\widehat{\mathbf H}$,
  1453→and hence to approximability by finite-index right congruences with vanishing
  1454→gap.
  1455→\end{remark}
  1456→
  1457→The profinite completion is not an analytic Hilbert-space completion such as
  1458→an $L^2$ completion.  It is a logical or Stone-type completion generated by
  1459→finite deterministic quotients.

  1474→\subsection{Separated Task Theories}
  1475→\label{subsec:separated}
  1476→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1477→
  1478→\begin{definition}[Separated Task Theory]
  1479→\label{def:separated-task}
  1480→A task theory $\mathbb T$ is \textbf{separated} if:
  1481→\[
  1482→\mathbf R(X,Y)=\mathbf R(Y,X)=0
  1483→\implies
  1484→X=Y,
  1485→\]
  1486→and
  1487→\[
  1488→\mathcal E(X,Y)=0
  1489→\implies
  1490→X=Y.
  1491→\]
  1492→\end{definition}
  1493→
  1494→\begin{definition}[Nerode Equivalence]
  1495→\label{def:nerode}
  1496→For any task theory $\mathbb T$, the \textbf{Nerode equivalence}
  1497→$\sim_\delta$ is
  1498→\[
  1499→u\sim_\delta v
  1500→\iff
  1501→\forall w\in\mathcal I^*,
  1502→\quad
  1503→\mathbf R(\delta(uw),\delta(vw))
  1504→=
  1505→\mathbf R(\delta(vw),\delta(uw))
  1506→=
  1507→0.
  1508→\]
  1509→\end{definition}
  1510→
  1511→The two-sided condition is used because $\mathbf R$ need not be symmetric.  If
  1512→$\mathbf R$ is symmetric, the condition reduces to
  1513→\[
  1514→\mathbf R(\delta(uw),\delta(vw))=0
  1515→\quad
  1516→\forall w.
  1517→\]
  1518→
  1519→The Nerode equivalence is always a right congruence.  Indeed, if
  1520→$u\sim_\delta v$, then for every $x\in\mathcal I$ and every
  1521→$w\in\mathcal I^*$,
  1522→\[
  1523→\delta(uxw)=\delta(u(xw)),
  1524→\qquad
  1525→\delta(vxw)=\delta(v(xw)),
  1526→\]
  1527→so the defining zero-distance condition for $u$ and $v$ applied to the
  1528→continuation $xw$ gives $ux\sim_\delta vx$.
  1529→
  1530→In the commitment regime, $\sim_\delta$ is the usual Myhill--Nerode residual
  1531→equivalence.  In the retention regime, when predictive distributions are
  1532→distinct on the stationary support, it coincides with causal-state
  1533→equivalence.  In grounding regimes, it may correspond to equality of residual
  1534→kernels or impulse-response functionals, depending on the chosen residual
  1535→category.
  1536→

  1538→\subsection{Compact Uniform Task Theories}
  1539→\label{subsec:compact-uniform}
  1540→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1541→
  1542→\begin{definition}[Compact Metrizable $\mathbf V$-Category]
  1543→\label{def:compact-metrizable}
  1544→A $\mathbf V$-category $\mathbf R$ is \textbf{compact metrizable} if
  1545→$\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)$ carries a compact metric topology and the
  1546→hom-object map
  1547→\[
  1548→\mathbf R(-,-):
  1549→\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)\times\operatorname{ob}(\mathbf R)
  1550→\to
  1551→[0,\infty]
  1552→\]
  1553→is continuous in the metric topology, with the usual extended-real topology on
  1554→$[0,\infty]$.
  1555→\end{definition}
  1556→
  1557→More generally, one may use a compact uniform space in place of a compact
  1558→metric space.  The profinite convergence meta-theorem is stated under the
  1559→hypothesis that $\mathbf R$ is compact metrizable or compact uniform and that
  1560→the cost profunctor $\mathcal E$ is uniformly continuous and separated.
  1561→
  1562→\begin{remark}[Separate Continuity Is Insufficient]
  1563→\label{rem:separate-continuity-insufficient}
  1564→Separate continuity alone is insufficient for the profinite equivalence.  The
  1565→meta-theorem uses joint uniform continuity, or compact-uniform continuity, of
  1566→the cost profunctor.
  1567→\end{remark}

  1569→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1570→\subsection{Hilbert-Module Task Theories and Linear Hankel Gaps}
  1571→\label{subsec:hilbert-module}
  1572→%----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1573→
  1574→\begin{definition}[Hilbert-Module Task Theory]
  1575→\label{def:hilbert-module}
  1576→A \textbf{Hilbert-module task theory} is a separated task theory on a finite
  1577→joint alphabet
  1578→\[
  1579→\Sigma=\mathcal I\times\mathcal O
  1580→\]
  1581→equipped with the following additional data:
  1582→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  1583→\item a Banach space $B$ of residual observables;
  1584→\item a bounded linear functional
  1585→\[
  1586→\phi:B\to\mathbb C;
  1587→\]
  1588→\item an impulse-response function
  1589→\[
  1590→h:\Sigma^*\to\mathbb C
  1591→\]
  1592→obtained from the true trajectory by
  1593→\[
  1594→h(u)=\phi(\delta(u));
  1595→\]
  1596→\item a Hankel operator
  1597→\[
  1598→H_\delta:\ell^2(\Sigma^*)\to\ell^2(\Sigma^*)
  1599→\]
  1600→specified on the standard basis by
  1601→\[
  1602→(H_\delta e_u)(v)=h(uv),
  1603→\]
  1604→\emph{provided} this densely defined formula extends to a bounded operator on
  1605→$\ell^2(\Sigma^*)$.  Boundedness is not automatic: for general $h$ the vector
  1606→$v\mapsto h(uv)$ need not be square summable, and even when it is, the induced
  1607→matrix need not be bounded.  A sufficient condition is the geometric decay
  1608→hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:stable-decay}, which yields boundedness by
  1609→the Schur test;
  1610→\item compactness of the resulting bounded operator $H_\delta$.
  1611→\end{enumerate}
  1612→A Hilbert-module task theory is said to be \emph{spectrally admissible} when
  1613→the Hankel operator exists as a bounded operator and is compact, i.e.\ when
  1614→conditions~(iv) and~(v) hold.  All singular-value statements below are
  1615→asserted only for spectrally admissible task theories.
  1616→\end{definition}
  1617→
  1618→If the residual objects are operators, $B$ may be an operator space or a
  1619→$C^*$-algebra, and $\phi$ may be a bounded functional selecting the scalar
  1620→observable relevant to the impulse response.  The scalar impulse response $h$
  1621→is the object seen by the Hankel operator.  Distinct residual objects may
  1622→induce the same impulse response; the Hankel theory below depends only on $h$
  1623→unless additional domination hypotheses are supplied.
  1624→
  1625→\begin{remark}[Residual Norm versus Hankel Norm]
  1626→\label{rem:hilbert-residual-hankel}
  1627→The norm used for residual-operator comparisons is the norm of $B$, while the
  1628→Hankel gap uses the $\ell^2$ operator norm of $H_\delta$.  No domination
  1629→between the $B$-norm and the Hankel operator norm is assumed.
  1630→\end{remark}
  1631→
  1632→The model does not assume an unqualified domination hypothesis of the form
  1633→\[
  1634→\norm{H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1635→\le
  1636→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta).
  1637→\]
  1638→Such an inequality may hold under explicit decay or summability conditions,
  1639→but it is not a general axiom.
  1640→
  1641→\begin{definition}[Unrestricted Linear Hankel Relaxation Gap]
  1642→\label{def:linear-hankel-gap}
  1643→The \textbf{unrestricted linear Hankel $M$-rank relaxation gap} is
  1644→\[
  1645→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)
  1646→=
  1647→\inf_{\operatorname{rank}\widehat H\le M}
  1648→\norm{H_\delta-\widehat H}_{\mathrm{op}}.
  1649→\]
  1650→The infimum is over bounded operators $\widehat H$ of rank at most $M$, not
  1651→necessarily Hankel operators.
  1652→\end{definition}
  1653→
  1654→This is an operator-approximation gap.  It is the object to which
  1655→Eckart--Young--Mirsky \cite{eckart1936,mirsky1960} applies directly.
  1656→
  1657→\begin{definition}[Hankel-Structured Finite-Rank Gap]
  1658→\label{def:hankel-structured-gap}
  1659→The \textbf{Hankel-structured finite-rank gap} is
  1660→\[
  1661→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{Hank,str}}(M)
  1662→=
  1663→\inf_{\substack{
  1664→\operatorname{rank}\widehat H\le M\\
  1665→\widehat H\ \text{Hankel}
  1666→}}
  1667→\norm{H_\delta-\widehat H}_{\mathrm{op}}.
  1668→\]
  1669→The infimum is over Hankel operators of rank at most $M$.
  1670→\end{definition}
  1671→
  1672→Because the Hankel-structured feasible set is a subset of the unrestricted
  1673→feasible set,
  1674→\[
  1675→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{Hank,str}}(M)
  1676→\ge
  1677→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M).
  1678→\]
  1679→Equality requires additional structure.
  1680→
  1681→\begin{definition}[Linear Finite-Rank Residual-Cost Gap]
  1682→\label{def:lin-finite-rank-gap}
  1683→The \textbf{linear finite-rank residual-cost gap} is
  1684→\[
  1685→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{lin}}(M)
  1686→=
  1687→\inf
  1688→\left\{
  1689→\mathcal L_\infty(\delta,\widehat\delta):
  1690→\operatorname{rank}H_{\widehat\delta}\le M
  1691→\right\}.
  1692→\]
  1693→\end{definition}
  1694→
  1695→The residual-cost gap $\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{lin}}(M)$ and the
  1696→unrestricted Hankel relaxation gap
  1697→$\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)$ are distinct objects.  The former
  1698→measures task loss of finite-rank residual approximants; the latter measures
  1699→operator-norm approximation to $H_\delta$.  They are related only under
  1700→explicit domination or achievability hypotheses.
  1701→
  1702→\begin{definition}[Weighted Residual Norm]
  1703→\label{def:weighted-residual-norm}
  1704→For $0<\rho<1$, define
  1705→\[
  1706→\norm{d}_{\infty,\rho}
  1707→=
  1708→\sup_{w\in\Sigma^*}
  1709→\rho^{-|w|}
  1710→|d(w)|.
  1711→\]
  1712→\end{definition}

  1714→\begin{proposition}[Stable-Decay Hankel Domination]
  1715→\label{prop:stable-decay}
  1716→Suppose
  1717→\[
  1718→|h_\delta(w)-h_{\widehat\delta}(w)|
  1719→\le
  1720→\varepsilon\rho^{|w|}
  1721→\]
  1722→with
  1723→\[
  1724→0<\rho<\frac1{|\Sigma|}.
  1725→\]
  1726→Then
  1727→\[
  1728→\norm{H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1729→\le
  1730→\frac{1}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}
  1731→\varepsilon.
  1732→\]
  1733→Equivalently, if
  1734→\[
  1735→\norm{h_\delta-h_{\widehat\delta}}_{\infty,\rho}
  1736→\le
  1737→\varepsilon,
  1738→\]
  1739→then
  1740→\[
  1741→\norm{H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1742→\le
  1743→\frac{1}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}
  1744→\norm{h_\delta-h_{\widehat\delta}}_{\infty,\rho}.
  1745→\]
  1746→\end{proposition}
  1747→
  1748→\begin{proof}
  1749→Let
  1750→\[
  1751→A=H_\delta-H_{\widehat\delta}.
  1752→\]
  1753→The Hankel matrix entries satisfy
  1754→\[
  1755→|A(u,v)|
  1756→=
  1757→|h_\delta(uv)-h_{\widehat\delta}(uv)|
  1758→\le
  1759→\varepsilon\rho^{|u|+|v|}.
  1760→\]
  1761→By Schur's test,
  1762→\[
  1763→\sup_u\sum_v |A(u,v)|
  1764→\le
  1765→\varepsilon
  1766→\sup_u
  1767→\rho^{|u|}
  1768→\sum_{v\in\Sigma^*}
  1769→\rho^{|v|}.
  1770→\]
  1771→Since $\rho^{|u|}\le1$ and the number of words of length $n$ is
  1772→$|\Sigma|^n$,
  1773→\[
  1774→\sum_{v\in\Sigma^*}\rho^{|v|}
  1775→=
  1776→\sum_{n=0}^\infty
  1777→|\Sigma|^n\rho^n
  1778→=
  1779→\frac{1}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}.
  1780→\]
  1781→Thus
  1782→\[
  1783→\sup_u\sum_v |A(u,v)|
  1784→\le
  1785→\frac{\varepsilon}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}.
  1786→\]
  1787→The same bound holds for columns.  Schur's test therefore gives
  1788→\[
  1789→\norm{A}_{\mathrm{op}}
  1790→\le
  1791→\frac{\varepsilon}{1-|\Sigma|\rho}.
  1792→\]
  1793→\end{proof}

  1795→\begin{remark}[AAK versus EYM]
  1796→\label{rem:aak-eym-hilbert}
  1797→The default spectral converse for
  1798→$\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)$ is Eckart--Young--Mirsky:
  1799→\[
  1800→\Delta_{\mathbb T}^{\mathrm{unres}}(M)
  1801→=
  1802→\sigma_{M+1}(H_\delta).
  1803→\]
  1804→Adamjan--Arov--Krein applies only after a Hardy-space embedding has been
  1805→separately established.  The model does not assume such an embedding by
  1806→default.

  1809→\begin{remark}[Infinite support and boundedness]
  1810→\label{rem:infinite-support}
  1811→Infinite support of an impulse response does not by itself imply that the
  1812→associated Hankel operator is unbounded.  Boundedness requires decay or
  1813→summability conditions.  For example, scalar impulse responses satisfying
  1814→$|h(u)|\le C\rho^{|u|}$ with $0<\rho<1/|\Sigma|$ define bounded Hankel-type
  1815→operators by the Schur-test argument above.  Bounded symbolic impulse
  1816→responses that fail to decay may or may not define bounded Hankel operators;
  1817→boundedness is checked case by case.
  1818→\end{remark}

  1822→\label{subsec:controlled-markov}

  1824→
  1825→\begin{definition}[Stationary Controlled Causal Machine]
  1826→\label{def:controlled-markov}
  1827→A \textbf{stationary controlled causal machine} consists of:
  1828→\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  1829→\item a finite input alphabet $\mathcal I$;
  1830→\item a standard Borel output space $\mathcal O$;
  1831→\item a finite state set $\mathcal S$;
  1832→\item a deterministic transition map
  1833→\[
  1834→\tau:\mathcal S\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal S;
  1835→\]
  1836→\item emission laws
  1837→\[
  1838→P_s\in\mathcal P(\mathcal O)
  1839→\]
  1840→for each $s\in\mathcal S$;
  1841→\item a stationary ergodic input process $(X_t)_{t\in\mathbb Z}$;
  1842→\item a stationary state process $(S_t)_{t\in\mathbb Z}$ satisfying
  1843→\[
  1844→S_t=\tau(S_{t-1},X_t)
  1845→\quad\text{a.s.};
  1846→\]
  1847→\item a stationary distribution
  1848→\[
  1849→\pi_s=\mathbb P(S_t=s);
  1850→\]
  1851→\item a predictive future variable $Z_t$, by default the next output $Y_t$,
  1852→such that $S_t$ is sufficient:
  1853→\[
  1854→Z_t\perp\!\!\!\perp
  1855→\{S_u,X_u,Y_u:u<t\}
  1856→\mid S_t.
  1857→\]
  1858→\end{enumerate}
  1859→The predictive distribution of state $s$ is
  1860→\[
  1861→P_s=\mathcal L(Z_t\mid S_t=s).
  1862→\]
  1863→The stationary support is
  1864→\[
  1865→\Splus=\{s\in\mathcal S:\pi_s>0\}.
  1866→\]
  1867→\end{definition}
  1868→
  1869→\begin{definition}[$Z$-predictive equivalence]
  1870→\label{def:z-predictive-equivalence}
  1871→Fix the predictive target $Z_t$.  On the stationary support define
  1872→\[
  1873→s\sim_Z s'
  1874→\iff
  1875→\mathcal L(Z_t\mid S_t=s)
  1876→=
  1877→\mathcal L(Z_t\mid S_t=s').
  1878→\]
  1879→If $Z_t$ is the entire controlled future, $\sim_Z$ is causal-state
  1880→equivalence.  If $Z_t=Y_t$ is only the next output, $\sim_Z$ is one-step
  1881→predictive equivalence, which is strictly coarser in general and need not
  1882→identify causal states.
  1883→
  1884→Throughout, ``causal equivalence'' means $\sim_Z$ for the declared target
  1885→$Z$, and coincides with causal-state equivalence exactly when $Z$ is the full
  1886→future.  All retention statements, including the zero-retention threshold,
  1887→are to be read with respect to distinct $Z$-predictive states.
  1888→
  1889→For unifilar machines with input-dependent emissions
  1890→(Definition~\ref{def:unifilar-machine}) the predictive object attached to a
  1891→state is the whole kernel $x\mapsto P_s^x$, and $\sim_Z$ compares kernels on
  1892→the input support: $s\sim_Zs'$ if and only if $P_s^x=P_{s'}^x$ for every input
  1893→$x$ of positive mass.
  1894→\end{definition}
  1895→
  1896→Finite output alphabets are the special case in which all $P_s$ are supported
  1897→on a finite set.  Gaussian quadratic retention is obtained by taking
  1898→$\mathcal O=\mathbb R^D$ and $P_s$ Gaussian.
  1899→
  1900→\begin{definition}[Synchronized Finite-History Realization]
  1901→\label{def:synchronized-realization}
  1902→A stationary controlled causal machine has \textbf{synchronization depth}
  1903→$L<\infty$ if there exists a surjection
  1904→\[
  1905→\sigma_L:\mathcal I^L\to\Splus
  1906→\]
  1907→such that, almost surely,
  1908→\[
  1909→S_t=\sigma_L(X_{t-L+1},\dots,X_t).
  1910→\]
  1911→For histories $u$ with $|u|\ge L$, define
  1912→\[
  1913→\sigma(u)=\sigma_L(\operatorname{suffix}_L(u)).
  1914→\]
  1915→Then for all sufficiently long $u$,
  1916→\[
  1917→\sigma(ux)=\tau(\sigma(u),x).
  1918→\]
  1919→The finite-history trajectory is
  1920→\[
  1921→\delta(u)=P_{\sigma(u)}.
  1922→\]
  1923→\end{definition}
  1924→
  1925→Thus the original finite-history/right-congruence syntax is recovered for
  1926→definite and synchronizing machines, including all NP-hardness constructions.
  1927→
  1928→\begin{definition}[Causal Equivalence]
  1929→\label{def:causal-equivalence}
  1930→On the stationary support, causal equivalence is
  1931→\[
  1932→s\sim_\delta t
  1933→\iff
  1934→P_s=P_t.
  1935→\]
  1936→In a synchronized machine, the corresponding history equivalence is
  1937→\[
  1938→u\sim_\delta v
  1939→\iff
  1940→\sigma(u)=\sigma(v).
  1941→\]
  1942→If the predictive distributions $P_s$ are pairwise distinct on $\Splus$, then
  1943→causal equivalence coincides with state identity on $\Splus$.
  1944→\end{definition}
  1945→
  1946→\begin{definition}[Lumpable Quotient]
  1947→\label{def:lumpable-quotient}
  1948→A map $\phi:\Splus\to\mathcal K$ is \textbf{lumpable} if there exists a
  1949→deterministic quotient transition
  1950→\[
  1951→\tau_{\mathcal K}:\mathcal K\times\mathcal I\to\mathcal K
  1952→\]
  1953→such that
  1954→\[
  1955→\phi(\tau(s,x))
  1956→=
  1957→\tau_{\mathcal K}(\phi(s),x)
  1958→\]
  1959→for all $s\in\Splus$ and all inputs $x$ in the support of the input process.
  1960→For worst-case aggregation, require this for all $x\in\mathcal I$.
  1961→The quotient process is
  1962→\[
  1963→K_t=\phi(S_t).
  1964→\]
  1965→\end{definition}
  1966→
  1967→\begin{proposition}[Right-Congruence Correspondence in Synchronized Machines]
  1968→\label{prop:lumpability}
  1969→Let the machine have synchronization depth $L$.  A lumpable quotient
  1970→$\phi:\Splus\to\mathcal K$ induces a right congruence on sufficiently long
  1971→histories by
  1972→\[
  1973→u\sim_\phi v
  1974→\iff
  1975→\phi(\sigma(u))=\phi(\sigma(v)).
  1976→\]
  1977→Conversely, assume that $Z$-predictive equivalence $\sim_Z$ of
  1978→Definition~\ref{def:z-predictive-equivalence} is itself a right congruence on
  1979→$\Splus$, i.e.\ $s\sim_Zs'$ implies $\tau(s,x)\sim_Z\tau(s',x)$ for every
  1980→feasible input $x$.  This holds in particular when $Z$ is the full controlled
  1981→future, so that $\sim_Z$ is causal-state equivalence.  Then any finite-index
  1982→right congruence coarser than $\sim_Z$ induces a lumpable quotient of
  1983→$\Splus$.
  1984→
  1985→The hypothesis cannot be dropped: by Example~\ref{ex:onestep-not-congruence},
  1986→one-step predictive equivalence need not be a right congruence, and the
  1987→induced partition need not be lumpable.
  1988→\end{proposition}
